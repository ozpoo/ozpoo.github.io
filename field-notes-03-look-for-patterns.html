Field Notes — Look for Patterns

Field Notes: Working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.

I say: “Look for patterns. That’s where you find answers.”

But why?

What is it about patterns that makes them useful?

Patterns have:

  • relationship
  • repeatability
  • observability

Maybe patterns are the product of forces acting against entropy.

Or maybe that’s not quite right.

I keep thinking about something Bernard says in Westworld:

“That which is real is irreplaceable.”

I don’t know if that’s actually a philosophical proposition, or just a line that happens to sound like one.

But it stuck with me.

Because I’m not sure I agree with it.

Or maybe I don’t yet understand why it feels true.

For me to recognize something as real, it seems like it has to be repeatable in some sense.

Not necessarily identical.

But consistent.

It has to continue to play by recognizable rules.

A thing happens once, and I can call it an event.

But when something continues to happen according to the same relationships, I start calling it reality.

When something falls outside those expectations, I become uncertain.

Is the thing wrong?

Is my model wrong?

Am I misunderstanding what I’m seeing?

Maybe our models of reality are built from patterns.

We observe something repeatedly.

We identify a relationship.

We build a model.

The model gives us expectations.

Then we use those expectations to determine whether new observations make sense.

So:

observation → pattern → model → expectation

And perhaps our experience of reality depends, at least partially, on that loop.

But then I run into a problem.

Patterns are repeatable.

And if something is repeatable, doesn’t that mean it is replaceable?

If I can reproduce the pattern, why would the original instance be irreplaceable?

Maybe that’s where identity enters.

Two things can have the same pattern without being the same thing.

A copy of an object can reproduce its structure.

But it cannot reproduce its history.

I can make another identical chair.

I cannot make that chair’s history happen again.

Maybe:

pattern describes what can be repeated.

identity describes what cannot.

And that makes the word “real” feel strange.

Maybe something can be reproducible as a pattern while remaining irreplaceable as an instance.

A particular event happened at a particular place and time.

Even if I recreate every condition that produced it, I haven’t recreated that event.

I’ve created another event with the same pattern.

So perhaps the distinction isn’t:

real vs. reproducible

but:

pattern vs. instance.

And then I start thinking about quantum mechanics.

Not because I understand quantum mechanics well enough to make a claim here.

But because the basic idea seems to destabilize some of the assumptions I’ve been making about states, observation, predictability, and identity.

I need to be careful here.

Maybe the interesting question isn’t whether observation makes something real.

Maybe it’s:

What does it mean for something to have a determinate identity when our description of it is probabilistic?

I don’t know where this goes yet.

But I think there is something here.

Maybe we look for patterns because patterns make the world intelligible.

But maybe the world is never identical to the patterns we use to describe it.

Maybe literacy is partly the ability to distinguish the pattern from the instance.

And maybe technology keeps forcing us to move between those two levels.

A typeface is a pattern.

A particular letter printed on a particular piece of paper is an instance.

A design system is a pattern.

A particular interaction experienced by a particular person is an instance.

A software program is a pattern.

A particular execution of that program is an instance.

An AI model contains patterns.

A particular generated output is an instance.

Technology constantly moves between these levels.

So perhaps technological literacy isn’t simply knowing how to use a tool.

Perhaps it is learning to recognize:

what is a pattern, what is an instance, what rules produce the pattern, and where the instance can escape the model.

I need to sit with that.

Still figuring it out.
