A Way In — 04: The Default

A Way In: A series of public essays about what it means to live in a technological world — and what it might mean to become literate within it.

I have a question.

What is the difference between a choice

and a choice that has already been made for you?

Because I think I have been using the word choice incorrectly.

Or maybe—

I have been assuming that a choice only exists when I can see it.

Think about your phone.

You install something.

It asks:

Do you want to allow notifications?

Maybe you say yes.

Maybe you say no.

That’s a choice.

Pretty obvious.

But now imagine the same thing happens and the box is already checked.

You can still uncheck it.

Technically.

But the system has already answered the question for you.

You are not choosing whether to receive notifications.

You are choosing whether to change the choice.

That feels like a small distinction.

It isn’t.

A default is what happens

when nobody intervenes.

That might be the simplest definition I have found.

The font that is already selected.

The privacy setting that is already enabled.

The subscription that renews automatically.

The website that remembers your preferences.

The feed that decides what appears first.

The application that opens when you click a file.

The route your maps application suggests.

The option at the top of the list.

The button that is bigger.

The box that is already checked.

The thing that happens

because nothing else happened.

And this is where technology gets interesting.

Because defaults are everywhere.

Not just in software.

You don’t have to use a computer to encounter one.

There is a default size.

A default speed.

A default language.

A default form.

A default classroom.

A default schedule.

A default way of measuring something.

A default way of organizing information.

A default way of doing a job.

A default way of thinking about what the problem even is.

Somebody had to decide.

Or something happened repeatedly enough that eventually nobody remembered that it was a decision.

This is one of the strange things about technology.

We tend to notice the things it does.

We don’t always notice the things it assumes.

And assumptions can be incredibly powerful.

If a form asks for your information in a particular order,

it is making an assumption about what information matters.

If an application makes one action easier than another,

it is making an assumption about what you are supposed to do.

If a system requires you to opt out instead of opt in,

it is making an assumption about what should happen when you do nothing.

And if you encounter that system every day,

the assumption can start to feel like reality.

This is why I keep coming back to the word default.

Because a default doesn’t usually feel like a decision.

It feels like

the way things are.

And that is a very different thing.

Imagine walking into a room.

There are ten chairs.

Nine of them are pushed against the wall.

One is sitting in the middle of the room.

Nobody tells you where to sit.

Technically,

you can sit anywhere.

But where do you think most people will sit?

Did anyone force you?

No.

Did anyone tell you what to do?

No.

But someone arranged the room.

And that arrangement changed the choice.

Now imagine that room is an interface.

Or a website.

Or a workplace.

Or a school.

Or a city.

Or an economy.

Suddenly the question gets much bigger.

What choices are actually available to me?

What choices are visible?

Which ones require extra work?

Which ones require permission?

Which ones are expensive?

Which ones are inconvenient?

Which ones are buried?

Which ones are already selected?

Which ones never appear at all?

And maybe the most interesting question is:

Who decided what happens when I don’t decide?

I don’t think this means defaults are bad.

In fact, defaults are incredibly useful.

I would not want to configure every setting on my phone every morning.

I don’t want to choose the language every time I open an application.

I don’t want my car to ask me which side of the road I should drive on.

I don’t want to rebuild the internet before sending an email.

Defaults make things usable.

They reduce friction.

They allow systems to function.

They are part of what makes technology disappear.

And that’s probably a good thing.

Until the default stops being helpful.

Or until we forget that it is a default at all.

Maybe that’s the part worth paying attention to.

Not:

Is this default good or bad?

But:

Is this actually a choice?

And:

Do I know that someone made it?

Because once you recognize a default,

something strange happens.

It becomes visible.

And once it becomes visible,

you can question it.

Maybe you keep it.

Maybe you change it.

Maybe you decide that it makes sense.

Maybe you decide that it doesn’t.

Maybe you discover that you can’t change it.

And that is useful information too.

Because now you know something about the system.

This is beginning to feel like another part of what I mean by technological literacy.

It isn’t just knowing how to operate something.

It is noticing the decisions hidden inside the operation.

It is being able to look at a system and ask:

Why is it this way?

Why is this the first option?

Why is this already selected?

Why is this easier?

Why is that harder?

Why do I have to ask?

Why don’t I?

What happens if I do nothing?

What happens if I refuse?

What happens if I choose something else?

And maybe there is another question underneath all of those.

One that I think matters a lot.

What happens when everyone accepts the default?

Because one person’s default might be almost meaningless.

But a default repeated across millions of people

becomes a pattern.

A pattern becomes behavior.

Behavior becomes expectation.

Expectation becomes normal.

And eventually,

nobody remembers that there was ever another option.

Maybe that’s how some of the biggest decisions around us happen.

Not because someone stood up and said,

“This is what everyone should do.”

But because someone made one option slightly easier.

Then another.

And another.

And another.

Until the path was already there.

This is one of the reasons I am interested in technology as something humans construct.

Because if technology were simply there, there wouldn’t be much to ask.

But somebody built this.

Somebody organized it.

Somebody chose what would happen first.

Somebody decided what would require another click.

Somebody decided what would be visible.

Somebody decided what would happen when nobody did anything.

And sometimes those decisions were deliberate.

Sometimes they were inherited.

Sometimes they were accidents.

Sometimes they were made twenty years ago and nobody has revisited them.

Sometimes they were made because they were convenient.

Sometimes because they were profitable.

Sometimes because everyone else was already doing it.

Sometimes because nobody thought to ask.

Which means a default doesn’t necessarily tell us what somebody wanted.

But it does tell us something about how the system currently works.

And that gives us somewhere to begin.

So now I have another little exercise I keep wanting to try.

Look around.

Find a default.

Not a dramatic one.

A boring one.

Something you normally don’t even notice.

The way a door opens.

The way a form is organized.

The way your phone presents information.

The way a website asks you to agree.

The way your workplace measures something.

The way your classroom is arranged.

The way you buy something.

The way you get somewhere.

The way you communicate.

The way you spend your time.

Find the thing that happens

because nobody stopped it from happening.

And ask:

Who chose this?

Why?

What would happen if the default were different?

You don’t have to change it.

That’s not really the point.

The point is noticing that it could be different.

Because that tiny realization changes something.

The thing that felt inevitable

becomes constructed.

The thing that felt like reality

becomes a decision.

And the decision

becomes a question.

Maybe that’s another way in.

Not learning how to control technology.

Not learning every piece of software.

Not becoming an expert.

Just learning to notice the moment when something quietly says:

This is how we do it.

And having enough understanding to ask:

Do we have to?

Author’s Note

I think I keep finding myself interested in things that are hiding in plain sight.

The default is one of those things.

I started thinking about defaults as a fairly simple feature of technology: a setting that has already been chosen, an option that has already been selected, a thing that happens when nobody intervenes.

But the more I sat with it, the less simple it seemed.

Because a default isn’t really just a setting.

It is a little piece of a system’s logic.

It tells us what happens when we don’t act.

And that feels important.

I don’t think every default is intentional. I don’t think every default is manipulative. And I definitely don’t think the answer is to question every tiny decision we encounter until using a toaster becomes a philosophical exercise.

I’m more interested in the moment when something stops feeling like a decision.

When the way things are becomes indistinguishable from the way someone decided they should be.

That’s where this one started for me.

I am still trying to figure out what that means.

So, for now, I’m just trying to notice the defaults.

— Oz
