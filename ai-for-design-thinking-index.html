Oh yes. I think this could be a really nice third piece of the site because it gives the whole thing a slightly different function:

  • A Way In → questions / orientation
  • Field Notes → ongoing inquiry
  • AI for Design Thinking → things to try

And I’d resist making the third collection feel like an “AI tips” library. The more interesting framing is AI as something a designer can use to think with—a tool for getting outside your own head, testing assumptions, generating possibilities, and seeing a problem from another position.

The collection

AI for Design Thinking

A collection of small experiments for using AI as part of the design process.

AI can generate things.

But generation is only one part of designing.

These exercises explore other ways AI might participate in the work: as an audience, a critic, a research partner, a testing environment, a source of possibilities, or simply another perspective to think against.

The point isn’t to ask AI to design for you.

The point is to give yourself something to respond to.

Each guide is intentionally small. Try one when you’re stuck. Use one before a critique. Bring one into a studio day. Fold one into a project you’re already working on.

You make the decisions. AI gives you something to think about.

And then I think the individual guides should be really short. Almost like little field tools rather than articles.

⸻

I think there are several more in this same family that would make the collection feel substantial without becoming a giant resource library:

  • AI as a Brainstorm Partner — generate possibilities without asking it to choose.
  • AI as a User Tester — simulate different users encountering a design.
  • AI as a Research Partner — identify questions, assumptions, connections, and things to investigate.
  • AI as a Translator — explain the same concept to different audiences or in different forms.
  • AI as a Devil’s Advocate — deliberately attack a concept or expose its assumptions.
  • AI as a Metaphor Machine — generate unexpected conceptual connections.
  • AI as a Constraint — give AI strange limitations and see what those constraints reveal.
  • AI as a Pattern Finder — look across references, observations, or iterations for recurring ideas.
  • AI as a Reflection Partner — give it your process and ask it to help you identify what you’ve actually been doing/thinking.
  • AI as a Simulator — rehearse how a concept might behave in different contexts.

And there’s a nice underlying structure hiding in there:

AI doesn’t have to make the thing. It can help you see the thing.

That feels very aligned with the larger technology-literacy work you’re building: moving the conversation away from “What can AI generate?” and toward “What can I do with this technology to become a better thinker, designer, and decision-maker?”

⸻

And I think there’s a really nice editorial principle emerging across the whole collection:

AI can play different roles.

Audience → What does someone else see? Critique partner → What am I missing? Brainstorm partner → What else could this be? Devil’s advocate → Why might this fail? User tester → How might someone experience this? Translator → What is the idea underneath all the explanation? Metaphor machine → What happens if I look at this differently? Reflection partner → What am I actually doing?

That’s a much more interesting collection than “10 ways to use ChatGPT for graphic design.”

Because the underlying lesson is almost invisible:

You don’t need AI to make a design. You can use AI to make your thinking more visible.

And that feels like it belongs on your site.
