Technological Literacy

From knowing how to use technology to understanding how to participate in it.

What does it mean to understand the thing?

Status: Working framework
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

A part of—The Foundations:
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

THE PROBLEM WITH “KNOWING TECHNOLOGY”

We often describe someone as technologically literate when they can use technology effectively.

They know how to operate the software.

They can navigate the interface.

They know which button to press.

They can troubleshoot common problems.

They know the terminology.

They may even be exceptionally proficient.

But proficiency is not necessarily literacy.

A person can be extremely good at using a system without understanding the system itself.

Someone can drive a car without understanding an engine.

Someone can use a search engine without understanding how search results are produced.

Someone can use social media without understanding how recommendation systems shape what they encounter.

Someone can use generative AI without understanding what a language model is, what data it depends upon, what its limitations are, or why its outputs behave the way they do.

This is not a failure.

It is normal.

We live in a world of specialization. No one can understand everything they use.

The problem emerges when operational proficiency is mistaken for understanding.

We can become very good at operating systems we cannot meaningfully question.

LITERACY IS NOT MASTERY

Literacy is often misunderstood as knowing enough to be an expert.

It isn’t.

We do not require everyone to become a mechanic to be literate about automobiles.

We do not require everyone to become a civil engineer to understand infrastructure.

We do not require everyone to become a computer scientist to meaningfully engage with software.

Literacy exists between ignorance and mastery.

It is the ability to understand enough to participate meaningfully.

That distinction matters.

The goal is not:

Know everything.

The goal is:

Know enough to see what is happening.

Enough to ask questions.

Enough to recognize assumptions.

Enough to identify consequences.

Enough to distinguish a limitation from a choice.

Enough to recognize when expertise is needed.

Enough to know when to trust.

Enough to know when to question.

Enough to make informed decisions about participation.

Technological literacy is therefore not the accumulation of technical facts.

It is the development of capacity.

A WORKING DEFINITION

Technological literacy is the capacity to understand, question, navigate, and meaningfully participate in the technological systems that shape our lives.

There are four important words here.

Understand.

Question.

Navigate.

Participate.

Each represents a different level of relationship with technology.

01 — UNDERSTAND

What is happening?

Understanding begins with being able to see a system beyond its interface.

What is it made of?

What does it depend upon?

What is it doing?

What is being translated?

What assumptions does it contain?

What are its limitations?

What happens when I interact with it?

Understanding does not require complete technical knowledge.

It requires a meaningful mental model.

A person does not need to know every component of an automobile to understand that fuel, combustion, mechanical systems, roads, maintenance, and human decisions are all part of the larger system that makes driving possible.

Likewise, a person does not need to understand every mathematical operation inside a machine-learning model to understand that a model is trained on data, operates through computational infrastructure, produces probabilistic outputs, and exists within a larger technical and economic system.

The point is not knowing every detail.

The point is knowing what kind of thing you are dealing with.

02 — QUESTION

What should I accept, challenge, or investigate?

Understanding creates the possibility of questioning.

This is where literacy becomes more than knowledge.

A technologically literate person can ask:

Why does it work this way?

Who decided that?

What assumptions are embedded here?

What is this system optimizing for?

Who benefits?

Who bears the cost?

What alternatives exist?

What happens if we change it?

Questioning does not require cynicism.

It does not mean assuming every technology is harmful or that every institution is acting in bad faith.

It means recognizing that technological systems are constructed.

They contain decisions.

And constructed things can be examined.

03 — NAVIGATE

How do I operate within the system intentionally?

Understanding and questioning are not enough.

We still have to live with technology.

We work inside it.

We communicate through it.

We depend upon it.

We teach it.

We build with it.

We make decisions through it.

Technological literacy therefore includes the ability to navigate systems deliberately.

This means recognizing when a technology is useful.

Recognizing when it is inappropriate.

Recognizing when a limitation matters.

Recognizing when expertise is required.

Recognizing when a convenient default is not aligned with your goals.

Recognizing when participation is beneficial.

Recognizing when participation carries costs.

Navigation is not resistance.

It is intentional participation.

04 — PARTICIPATE

What can I do with my understanding?

The final movement is participation.

A technologically literate person is not merely a consumer of technology.

They can potentially become a participant in its development, adaptation, critique, and transformation.

They can modify.

They can create.

They can repair.

They can redesign.

They can teach.

They can refuse.

They can propose alternatives.

They can help others understand.

They can participate in decisions about how technology should be used.

This is where literacy begins to become agency.

Understanding changes the relationship between the person and the system.

The system is no longer simply something that happens to them.

It becomes something they can potentially act within.

THE LITERACY SPECTRUM

Technological literacy is therefore not binary.

It is not:

literate / illiterate.

It is a spectrum of increasingly meaningful relationships with a system.

One might:

Encounter
→ experience a technology without understanding it.

Operate
→ use it successfully.

Understand
→ develop a mental model of how it works.

Question
→ examine its assumptions, consequences, and purpose.

Navigate
→ make intentional choices about participation.

Modify
→ alter the system or its configuration.

Create
→ build new technological possibilities.

Teach
→ help others develop understanding.

Transform
→ participate in changing the larger system.

These stages are not strictly linear.

Someone may be highly capable in one dimension and inexperienced in another.

A person might understand a system technically but have little awareness of its social consequences.

Someone else might understand its cultural implications deeply while having little ability to modify its technical structure.

Literacy is therefore multidimensional.

THE STRANGE THING ABOUT MODERN TECHNOLOGY

There is something historically unusual about our relationship with contemporary technology.

Many technologies have become simultaneously:

more powerful

and

less visible.

A person can interact with extraordinary computational systems through an interface containing a handful of buttons.

Complexity has been hidden behind convenience.

This is one of the great achievements of interface design.

It is also one of its complications.

Good interfaces allow people to accomplish things without understanding the machinery beneath them.

That is often exactly what we want.

We do not want to understand the plumbing every time we turn on a faucet.

We do not want to understand electrical engineering every time we turn on a light.

We do not want to understand network protocols every time we send a message.

Abstraction is useful.

The problem is not abstraction.

The problem is abstraction without the ability to look underneath when looking becomes necessary.

Technological literacy provides that ability.

THE RIGHT TO LOOK UNDERNEATH

Perhaps one of the simplest ways to understand technological literacy is this:

You should be able to look underneath the interface.

Not necessarily literally.

Conceptually.

You should be able to ask what is behind the thing you are experiencing.

What makes it possible?

What choices created it?

What does it depend upon?

What does it hide?

What does it reveal?

What does it encourage?

What does it prevent?

What happens beyond the screen?

This is why technological literacy is not synonymous with computer literacy.

A person can learn to use hundreds of applications without developing this capacity.

Technological literacy is more fundamental.

It is a way of looking at systems.

LITERACY AS A RELATIONSHIP

There is another important distinction.

Literacy is not simply something a person possesses.

It describes a relationship between a person and something they are trying to understand.

We can be literate in one domain and inexperienced in another.

We can become more literate over time.

We can lose familiarity.

We can encounter something entirely new and begin again.

This makes technological literacy inherently developmental.

It is not a destination.

It is a capacity for becoming more capable of understanding.

That distinction becomes particularly important in technology because technologies change faster than any curriculum can fully capture them.

Teaching someone every tool is therefore unlikely to be enough.

The tools will change.

The interfaces will change.

The platforms will disappear.

The terminology will change.

The underlying capacity to investigate can remain.

TEACHING THE TOOL VS. TEACHING THE CAPACITY

This creates a fundamental pedagogical distinction.

We can teach:

How to use this technology.

Or we can teach:

How to understand technologies.

The first has immediate utility.

The second has durability.

This does not mean tools are unimportant.

Tools matter.

Technical skill matters.

Craft matters.

Software proficiency matters.

But if education stops at operation, students may become highly capable users without becoming capable participants.

A durable technological education therefore needs both:

technical fluency

and

technological literacy.

The first enables action.

The second enables understanding.

Together, they create capability.

LITERACY AND THE UNKNOWN

One of the most useful consequences of literacy is that it changes our relationship with unfamiliar things.

Without a method, encountering an unfamiliar technology can produce a familiar response:

I don’t know how this works.

That can quickly become:

Someone else understands this.

which can become:

I’ll trust whatever they tell me.

Technological literacy offers another path:

I don’t know how this works yet.

That final word matters.

Yet.

Not knowing becomes the beginning of inquiry rather than the end of it.

We can investigate.

We can ask.

We can find documentation.

We can experiment.

We can observe.

We can take the thing apart.

This is where technological literacy begins to intersect with learning itself.

LITERACY REQUIRES A WAY OF LEARNING

If technological literacy is the capacity to meaningfully understand and participate in technological systems, then an important question follows:

How does someone develop that capacity?

We can provide information.

We can teach terminology.

We can demonstrate tools.

We can explain systems.

But information alone does not necessarily produce understanding.

Understanding often develops through interaction.

Through questioning.

Through experimentation.

Through failure.

Through comparison.

Through reconstruction.

Through making.

And, perhaps most fundamentally:

Through taking things apart.

THE BEGINNING OF A METHOD

Taking something apart is not inherently the same thing as understanding it.

A pile of components is not an explanation.

But decomposition changes the problem.

Instead of asking:

How does this whole thing work?

we can ask:

What are its parts?

Then:

What does each part do?

Then:

How are the parts related?

Then:

What happens if one changes?

The system becomes something we can investigate.

The unknown becomes a collection of questions.

This is the beginning of the next Foundation.

QUESTIONS THIS FOUNDATION OPENS

  • What is the minimum understanding necessary for technological literacy?
  • Can someone be technologically literate without being technically skilled?
  • Can someone be technically skilled without being technologically literate?
  • How does literacy change across different technologies?
  • How much should technological literacy be taught explicitly?
  • What should education prioritize when technologies change faster than curricula?
  • How does abstraction help and hinder understanding?
  • When does convenience become a barrier to literacy?
  • What role does experimentation play in developing understanding?
  • Can technological literacy be taught as a generalizable capacity rather than as knowledge of particular tools?
  • What happens when literacy becomes sufficient to alter the system itself?
  • If understanding creates agency, what responsibilities follow from that agency?

These questions lead to a practical one:

If we want people to become capable of understanding unfamiliar systems, how might we teach them to begin?

Perhaps we begin the same way we have always begun when confronted with something we do not understand.

We open it.

We separate the pieces.

We look.

We ask questions.

We try to put it back together.

Let’s take it apart.

Still figuring it out.
