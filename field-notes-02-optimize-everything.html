Field Notes — Optimize Everything

Field Notes: Working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.

In my work, the goals change with every project.

That’s always been part of the fun.

Different clients.

Different industries.

Different places.

Different problems.

But there always seems to be one goal that shows up to every party.

I’ve chased it in so many situations that, for better or worse, I’ve become intimately acquainted with its desires.

That goal is optimization.

So much technological development seems organized around optimization.

And just like that same goal keeps showing up everywhere I go, it keeps leading me to the same question:

Why are we optimizing?

Not:

Can we optimize this?

Not:

How much can we optimize it?

But:

Why this?

And toward what?

Because I’ve encountered situations where optimization seems to produce outcomes that are difficult to reconcile with the reason we wanted the optimization in the first place.

Something gets faster.

But worse.

Something gets cheaper.

But more harmful.

Something becomes more efficient.

But less human.

Something scales.

But becomes harder to control.

And I keep wondering:

If there are ways to improve a system that are more aligned with what we actually want…

why wouldn’t we choose those?

Faster.

Cheaper.

More efficient.

More scalable.

More productive.

Less labor.

More output.

Higher engagement.

Lower cost.

More convenience.

None of these are inherently bad goals.

They’re often useful.

Sometimes they’re extremely useful.

But have we quietly allowed them to become the goals?

Technology is frequently justified because it makes something faster.

But should everything be faster?

Automation is justified because it reduces labor.

But is labor itself always the problem?

Efficiency is justified because it reduces cost.

But whose cost?

Convenience is justified because it makes life easier.

But easier toward what?

Maybe optimization isn’t the problem.

Maybe the problem is what we’ve decided is worth optimizing.

There was a time when I didn’t know what a KPI was.

Or really, how KPIs related to the broader idea of metrics.

Then they started to make a lot of sense to me.

A metric is useful because it gives you something you can observe.

Something you can measure.

Something you can compare.

Something you can respond to.

A good metric can tell you:

Are we moving toward what we said we wanted?

And that’s powerful.

A measurable goal gives a person—or a team, or an organization, or a technological system—something to aim at.

You can make a decision.

Measure the result.

Respond.

Make another decision.

Measure again.

The system learns where it is relative to the goal.

And then I start to see the connection.

If you want to optimize something…

you need something to optimize against.

You need a target.

And a target needs some way of being recognized.

A metric provides that recognition.

So:

goal → metric → measurement → response → optimization

And the more I think about that loop, the more important the metric starts to feel.

Because the metric isn’t just telling us what happened.

It’s telling us what counts as success.

If I tell a system:

maximize speed

it will find ways to become faster.

If I tell it:

minimize cost

it will find ways to become cheaper.

If I tell it:

maximize engagement

it will find ways to increase engagement.

And this seems almost embarrassingly obvious.

Of course it does.

That’s what we asked it to do.

But then why do the outcomes sometimes surprise us?

Maybe because we’ve become very good at defining what we want to measure…

without being equally good at defining what we actually want.

A metric is a strange thing.

It looks objective.

A number feels clean.

Verifiable.

Defensible.

We can put it on a dashboard.

We can make a graph.

We can compare this quarter to last quarter.

We can say:

the number went up.

Therefore:

we improved.

But improved according to what?

And what isn’t represented by the number?

What did we leave outside the measurement?

What became invisible because we didn’t give it a metric?

Maybe this is where optimization gets interesting.

Because optimization doesn’t really care what we value.

It cares about the target we’ve given it.

And if the target is measurable…

the system can move toward it.

Very effectively.

Potentially much more effectively than we can.

Which means the real power might not be in optimization itself.

It might be in choosing the thing to optimize.

And then I start seeing something that feels almost like a loophole.

If optimization follows the metric…

and the metric defines the target…

then changing the metric changes the direction of the system.

Not necessarily the technology.

Not necessarily the infrastructure.

Not necessarily the people.

Maybe sometimes all you have to change is what counts as success.

What if a hospital doesn’t optimize for throughput?

What if it optimizes for patient outcomes?

What if a school doesn’t optimize for test performance?

What if it optimizes for long-term learning?

What if a social platform doesn’t optimize for time spent?

What if it optimizes for something closer to meaningful participation?

What if a business doesn’t optimize simply for quarterly revenue?

What else might it measure?

What would become visible?

What decisions would suddenly make sense?

What would become worth protecting?

What would become worth building?

This doesn’t mean that changing a metric magically solves a system.

It doesn’t.

Metrics can be poorly designed.

Goals can conflict.

Measurements can be gamed.

Systems have inertia.

And changing what we measure doesn’t erase the consequences of everything that came before.

But it does make me wonder about something.

Maybe we spend too much time asking:

How do we change the technology?

When sometimes the more consequential question is:

What are we asking the technology to optimize for?

Because once a system has a target, it starts looking for a path.

And if we change the target…

the path can change.

That feels strangely powerful.

Almost too simple.

A technological system doesn’t necessarily need a different capability to produce a different outcome.

Sometimes it needs a different definition of success.

And maybe that’s one of the places where human agency lives inside technological systems.

Not just in deciding whether to use a technology.

Not just in deciding how to use it.

But in deciding:

What are we actually trying to make happen?

How will we know if we’ve succeeded?

What are we willing to measure?

What are we unwilling to reduce to a number?

And perhaps most importantly:

What happens when we optimize for the wrong thing?

Still figuring it out.
