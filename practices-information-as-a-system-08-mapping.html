Mapping

Understanding place

Information as a System: Guides for investigating, organizing, and making sense of information.

The question: Where does this information exist in relation to something else?

What it is

Mapping organizes information according to location or spatial relationship.

A map does not have to represent geography literally. It can also represent conceptual, experiential, or relational space.

Works well with

  • Places
  • Buildings
  • Neighborhoods
  • Cities
  • Journeys
  • Environmental information
  • Locations of events
  • Spatial experiences

Try it

  1. Identify the information that has a spatial component.
  2. Determine what location means in your investigation.
  3. Establish a spatial framework.
  4. Place the information.
  5. Identify clusters, gaps, paths, and boundaries.
  6. Add relevant relationships.
  7. Test whether the spatial structure reveals something meaningful.
  8. Revise.

Look for

  • Clusters
  • Boundaries
  • Distance
  • Density
  • Paths
  • Gaps
  • Concentrations
  • Movement

Case study

Mapping a neighborhood

You might map:

  • Restaurants
  • Bus stops
  • Parks
  • Schools
  • Public art
  • Businesses
  • Pedestrian traffic

A conventional map shows where things are.

But changing the information can reveal something else:

  • Where are people?
  • Where do they move?
  • Where are services concentrated?
  • Where are there gaps?

The map becomes an investigative tool.
