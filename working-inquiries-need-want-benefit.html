Need, Want, Benefit

What happens when we distinguish between what is needed, what is wanted, and what is beneficial within a technological system?

Status: Early inquiry
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

A part of—The Foundations:
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

There seems to be a meaningful distinction between need, want, and benefit.

At first, the distinction is almost trivial.

A child may want an ice cream cone.

They may not need an ice cream cone.

They may nevertheless benefit from eating one.

These statements describe different relationships to the same situation:

  • Need describes necessity in relation to a condition or goal.
  • Want describes desire or preference.
  • Benefit describes an advantageous consequence or perceived value.

The distinction becomes more interesting when applied to technology.

From individual desire to technological systems

Technology is expressed through systems, whether those systems are large or small.

The design and creation of a technological system involve decisions and choices. Those decisions are made in relation to goals, constraints, values, assumptions, and anticipated outcomes.

The resulting system then produces outcomes that may or may not correspond to the needs, wants, and perceived benefits of the people who encounter it.

This raises a question:

Whose need? Whose want? Whose benefit?

A system may be designed to satisfy one need while producing a benefit for another person or institution.

A designer may want one thing.

A user may want another.

An organization may define success according to an entirely different outcome.

The system sits between them.

A preliminary relationship

One possible model is:

Need → Goal → Choice → System → Outcome → Benefit

Alongside this is a human relationship:

Want → Choice → Use → Experience

These relationships may overlap, but they are not identical.

A need can exist without being wanted.

A want can exist without being necessary.

A benefit can exist without being intended.

An outcome can benefit one participant while creating a cost or constraint for another.

And a technological system can make some choices feel so ordinary or inevitable that the choices themselves become difficult to see.

Why this may matter to technological literacy

If technological literacy involves developing understanding, agency, responsibility, and participation, then recognizing these distinctions may be important.

A technologically literate person might ask:

  • What does this system actually require?
  • What do I want from it?
  • What goal was this system designed to achieve?
  • Whose goals shaped its design?
  • What choices are embedded in the system?
  • What constraints am I encountering?
  • What outcomes does the system produce?
  • Who benefits from those outcomes?
  • Who bears the costs?
  • Which aspects of the system are necessary, and which are simply decisions that could have been made differently?

This shifts technological literacy away from simply knowing how to use a technology.

It begins to involve understanding why a technology behaves the way it does, what decisions produced that behavior, and how those decisions affect our ability to act.

A question about agency

There may be an important relationship between need, want, and agency.

Agency requires some ability to distinguish:

what I need
from what I want
from what I have been told I need
from what a system makes available
from what someone else benefits from me choosing.

This distinction may be particularly important in technological environments because systems often present decisions as if they were simply the way things are.

An interface has defaults.

A platform has rules.

A form has required fields.

A workflow has prescribed steps.

An algorithm has priorities.

A tool has affordances and limitations.

None of these necessarily appear to the user as decisions. Yet they are often the result of decisions.

An example

Consider a university registration system.

The institution may need to manage enrollment.

It may want an efficient process.

A designer makes choices about the interface and workflow.

Those choices become part of the system.

The institution may benefit from efficiency.

The student, meanwhile, may simply want to register for a class.

The student’s experience of the system may involve prerequisites, holds, permissions, enrollment caps, deadlines, and other constraints.

The system is therefore not merely a neutral mechanism for accomplishing a task.

It is an expression of decisions about:

what matters, what is permitted, what is restricted, what is required, and what counts as success.

A possible literacy practice

Perhaps technological literacy involves learning to take apart these relationships.

When encountering a technological system, ask:

NEED
What is actually necessary?

WANT
What does each participant desire?

GOAL
What is the system trying to accomplish?

CHOICE
What decisions produced the system?

CONSTRAINT
What possibilities have been limited or removed?

OUTCOME
What actually happens when the system is used?

BENEFIT
Who benefits, and how?

This is not yet proposed as a finished framework.

It is a possible analytical lens.

What remains unresolved

Several questions need testing.

Is need / want / benefit actually the right distinction, or are these simply entry points into a larger model?

Are goal, choice, constraint, and outcome necessary additions?

Can these relationships be identified consistently across different kinds of technologies?

Do they operate differently at different scales—from a physical object to an interface, platform, institution, or social system?

Can the model help students recognize decisions embedded in technologies without turning every technological system into an exercise in suspicion?

And perhaps most importantly:

Can distinguishing necessity, desire, choice, and benefit increase a person’s ability to understand and act within technological systems?

That is the question worth testing.

Current hypothesis

Technological literacy may require more than understanding how a system works. It may require recognizing the relationships between needs, wants, goals, choices, constraints, outcomes, and benefits—and recognizing that these relationships may belong to different people.

If that proves useful, this inquiry may eventually become a component of a larger model of technological literacy, agency, and systems understanding.

For now, it remains a thread worth pulling.
