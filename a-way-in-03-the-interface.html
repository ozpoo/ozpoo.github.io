A Way In — 03: The Interface

A Way In: A series of public essays about what it means to live in a technological world — and what it might mean to become literate within it.

You know that little trash can icon?

The one on your computer.

You probably don’t think about it very often.

You see it.

You know what it means.

You drag something onto it.

And the thing disappears.

Pretty simple.

Except—

what the hell is a trash can doing inside my computer?

There isn’t a little trash can in there.

There isn’t a tiny metal bin sitting somewhere inside my laptop,

waiting for me to throw a file into it.

So what am I actually looking at?

It’s an image.

A symbol.

A little picture that somebody designed

to mean something.

And somehow,

we all agreed.

That’s a trash can.

That’s kind of incredible.

Because the computer doesn’t need a trash can.

I do.

Or at least,

I need some way to tell the computer what I want.

And the computer needs some way to communicate back to me.

So somebody puts a little trash can between us.

And suddenly—

we can talk.

Maybe that’s what an interface is.

Not the thing itself.

Not the human.

But the thing between them.

A way for one system to become understandable to another.

A way for a person to tell a machine:

“Do this.”

And for the machine to respond:

“Okay.”

Or:

“I can’t.”

Or:

“You don’t have permission.”

Or—

sometimes—

“I have no idea what the hell you’re talking about.”

And we’ve gotten really good at making these things disappear.

Buttons.

Menus.

Icons.

Sliders.

Check boxes.

Search bars.

Notifications.

Swipe gestures.

Drag and drop.

Click.

Tap.

Scroll.

We learn them almost without realizing we’re learning them.

And once we do,

they become invisible.

We don’t think:

I am now interacting with a graphical user interface.

We think:

“I’m deleting this file.”

That’s the point.

A good interface gets out of the way.

But here’s where I start getting interested.

Because if an interface is the thing between me and the technology,

then the interface is also deciding

what I get to see.

What I get to do.

What I don’t get to do.

What seems possible.

What seems impossible.

What happens first.

What happens next.

What requires my attention.

What gets hidden.

What gets emphasized.

What gets ignored.

And suddenly that little trash can

doesn’t seem quite so simple.

Because somebody chose that trash can.

Somebody chose the size.

The shape.

The location.

The color.

The animation.

What happens when I click it.

What happens when I drag something onto it.

What happens when I change my mind.

Somebody decided whether there should be an “Undo.”

Somebody decided what happens when the trash is emptied.

Somebody decided whether I get a warning.

Somebody decided what that warning says.

Somebody decided what I am allowed to do.

And somebody decided what I am not allowed to do.

This is something I think we miss when we talk about technology.

We tend to think of the technology as the thing behind the interface.

The machine.

The software.

The algorithm.

The database.

The model.

The system.

And the interface feels like the friendly little layer on top.

The part designed to make everything easier.

But maybe the interface isn’t just making the technology easier to use.

Maybe it’s making a series of choices visible to us.

And sometimes—

it’s hiding them.

Think about a door.

A door is an interface.

A handle tells you:

pull.

A push bar tells you:

push.

A locked door tells you:

no.

And a door with no handle at all?

Well—

maybe you start wondering whether you’re supposed to be there.

The physical world is full of interfaces.

Road signs.

Crosswalks.

Elevator buttons.

Thermostats.

Vending machines.

Gas pumps.

A light switch.

A book.

A form.

A credit card reader.

Even the little paper manual that came with the toaster.

All of these things are ways of negotiating between a human being

and some larger system.

And then we built computers.

And suddenly the interface became a whole discipline.

We invented windows.

And folders.

And files.

And desktops.

And buttons.

And menus.

And suddenly the metaphor of a desktop became so familiar that most of us stopped thinking about how strange it is.

There isn’t a desktop.

There aren’t folders.

There aren’t files.

Not in the way we mean them.

They’re models.

Ideas.

Ways of giving us something familiar to hold onto while we interact with something profoundly unfamiliar.

And maybe that’s one of the most powerful things an interface can do.

It can make something complicated

feel understandable.

But that doesn’t necessarily mean we understand what is underneath it.

I can become incredibly good at using an interface

without understanding the technology behind it.

I can know exactly where to click

without knowing what happens when I click.

I can know what button to press

without knowing what system that button is connected to.

I can become fluent in the interface

while remaining completely illiterate in the technology.

And this is where I think about the toaster again.

Because maybe the toaster has an interface too.

The lever.

The dial.

The little buttons.

The numbers.

The little picture of toast.

Those things are telling me how the toaster wants me to understand it.

But imagine I replace those physical controls with a screen.

Now the toaster can show me

a menu.

A recommendation.

A notification.

Maybe it can learn my preferences.

Maybe it can predict when I want toast.

Maybe it can optimize something.

Maybe it can connect to the internet.

Maybe it has AI.

And suddenly my relationship with the toaster has changed.

Not necessarily because the toaster became more complicated.

But because the interface became the place where I encounter the complexity.

And that’s happening everywhere.

We’re increasingly interacting with systems

through interfaces that have been designed for us.

Search engines.

Social media.

Banking apps.

Maps.

Streaming services.

Online shopping.

Generative AI.

Recommendation systems.

Even the software we use to make things.

The interface isn’t simply showing us what the technology is.

It’s showing us

what the technology thinks we need to see.

And maybe that’s not a bad thing.

I don’t want to go back to a world where everyone has to understand every transistor.

I don’t want to inspect the source code every time I want to send an email.

I don’t want to understand the electrical engineering inside my microwave before I heat leftovers.

That’s not the point.

Interfaces are incredibly useful.

They allow us to work with systems that are vastly more complicated than we could ever hold in our heads.

That’s kind of their magic.

But maybe literacy means being able to recognize the magic trick.

Not necessarily knowing how every part works.

Just knowing that

there is a trick.

Because once I recognize that I’m interacting through an interface,

I can start asking questions.

Why am I being shown this?

Why is this button here?

Why is that option harder to find?

Why does this happen automatically?

Why do I have to agree before I can continue?

What happens if I don’t?

What information is the system asking me for?

What does it already know?

What choices am I being given?

What choices aren’t being shown?

And—

perhaps most importantly—

what happens outside the interface?

Because the interface can make a system feel small.

Manageable.

Friendly.

A little box.

A button.

A screen.

A trash can.

But the system behind it might be enormous.

There might be databases.

Servers.

Companies.

People.

Policies.

Algorithms.

Markets.

Incentives.

Machines.

Other systems.

Other people.

And I only see

the button.

Maybe that’s one of the strange things about living in a technological world.

We’re surrounded by interfaces designed to make enormous systems feel like little things we can touch.

And because they feel simple,

we assume the thing behind them must be simple too.

Maybe that’s where technological literacy starts to become really interesting.

Not with understanding everything behind the interface.

But with realizing:

there is something behind the interface.

And once I know that,

I can begin to wonder about it.

What is happening here?

What choices made this possible?

Who decided how I would interact with it?

What does the system want from me?

What does it allow me to do?

What does it prevent me from doing?

And what happens if I refuse to use the interface the way it expects?

Because maybe an interface isn’t just a way into technology.

Maybe it’s also

a way technology gets into us.

It teaches us how to behave.

It teaches us what to expect.

It teaches us what is normal.

It teaches us what counts as an option.

It can make some actions feel obvious

and others feel ridiculous.

It can make some choices easy

and others almost invisible.

And if we use an interface long enough,

we can forget that somebody made those choices.

We start to experience the interface

not as a design,

but as the way things are.

And I think that’s where I want to go next.

Because if an interface is a collection of choices about how we encounter a system—

what happens when some of those choices become invisible?

What happens when we stop noticing them?

What happens when the thing somebody chose for us

starts to feel like the thing that was always supposed to happen?

What happens when a choice

becomes

the default?

Maybe that’s the next question.

Not just:

What is the interface?

But:

Who chose what happens by default?

And maybe that’s where

things get really interesting.

Author’s Note

I keep coming back to the trash can.

Which is probably a little ridiculous.

It’s such a small thing.

A tiny picture that we’ve all somehow agreed means:

throw this away.

But the more I think about interfaces, the stranger that agreement starts to feel.

Because we spend so much of our lives interacting with things through little pictures, buttons, menus, gestures, forms, and screens.

And most of the time, that’s exactly what we want.

I don’t want to think about databases when I’m checking my bank account.

I don’t want to think about servers when I’m sending a message.

I don’t want to think about source code when I’m moving a file into the trash.

The interface is doing its job.

It lets me get on with my life.

But I think there’s something worth noticing in that convenience.

The interface doesn’t just help me interact with a system.

It determines how I get to encounter the system in the first place.

What I can see.

What I can do.

What I’m asked to decide.

What has already been decided for me.

And once I started noticing that,

I started seeing interfaces everywhere.

Which is, frankly, a little annoying.

Because now I can’t look at a button without wondering who decided it should be there.

I think that might be useful, though.

Not because we need to become suspicious of every button.

But because sometimes the first step toward understanding a technology

is simply realizing that there is something between us and it.

Something designed.

Something chosen.

Something made.

And if someone made it one way,

maybe it could have been made another way.

I think that might be where the next question begins.

— Oz

Still figuring it out.
