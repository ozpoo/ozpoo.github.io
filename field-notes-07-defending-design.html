Defending Design

Field Notes: Working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.Part of:

A part of—The Foundations:
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

I keep wondering why we defend design.

Critique.

Pin-up.

Presentation.

Defense.

These are ordinary parts of design education.

I have participated in them as a student. I have participated in them as a designer. Now I ask students to do the same thing.

Present the work.

Explain the decisions.

Be prepared to defend them.

And somehow, despite how fundamental this seems to design education, I don’t remember anyone ever really explaining why.

Not the practical why.

The deeper why.

Why do we expect a designer to be able to account for every decision?

Color.

Type.

Line weight.

Composition.

Form.

Image.

Dataset.

Interface placement.

Interaction.

Motion.

Behavior.

Why should a designer have a reason?

The obvious answer

There are easy answers.

Design solves problems.

Design serves people.

Design exists within constraints.

A designer makes intentional choices in response to those constraints.

So if I ask a student:

Why did you put that there?

I’m not necessarily asking for a philosophical defense of their existence.

I’m trying to determine whether the decision was intentional.

Did they understand the problem?

Did they consider alternatives?

Did they make a choice based on something?

Can they explain the relationship between the choice and the thing they’re trying to accomplish?

This makes sense.

If a designer cannot explain a decision, perhaps they don’t fully understand the decision themselves.

But then I started wondering if that explanation is too simple.

Because design decisions don’t just produce artifacts.

They produce experiences.

They organize information.

They establish relationships.

They direct attention.

They make some things visible and other things invisible.

They encourage some behaviors and discourage others.

They encode assumptions.

They participate in systems.

A seemingly small design decision can therefore have consequences well beyond the designer’s immediate intention.

And that changes the question.

What does “defend” actually mean?

There is something strange about the word.

We don’t simply say:

Explain your design.

We say:

Defend your design.

Defense implies that something is going to push back.

Someone will question the decision.

Someone will ask:

Why?

And the designer is expected to have an answer.

At first, I thought this was primarily about confidence.

Students need to learn how to talk about their work.

They need to present professionally.

They need to communicate with clients, collaborators, developers, researchers, stakeholders.

All true.

But perhaps critique is doing something more fundamental.

Perhaps we are teaching students a relationship to their own decisions.

You made this.

You made choices.

Those choices can be questioned.

You should understand those choices.

You should be able to account for them.

And perhaps, most importantly:

You should be willing to reconsider them.

Defense is not the same thing as justification

This distinction matters.

A student can become very good at defending a decision without actually understanding it.

“I chose blue because blue communicates trust.”

Okay.

Why?

“Because blue communicates trust.”

That isn’t really a rationale.

It is a design cliché.

Students can learn to produce explanations that sound like explanations.

This makes me wonder whether what we actually want isn’t defense at all.

Maybe what we want is accountability.

To account for a decision is different.

It might mean:

I know what I chose.

I know why I chose it.

I know what alternatives I considered.

I know what assumptions informed the choice.

I know what evidence I had.

I know what I don’t know.

I understand what the decision does.

I understand that it may affect people differently.

And I am willing to change it when I learn something that changes my understanding.

That feels much closer to what we are actually trying to teach.

Which brings me back to literacy

I’ve been thinking about technological literacy as the ability to understand enough about a technology or system to participate meaningfully within it.

Not simply knowing how to operate something.

Not simply knowing which button to press.

Understanding enough to make decisions.

Understanding enough to question.

Understanding enough to recognize assumptions.

Understanding enough to act.

And suddenly I wonder if design critique has been quietly practicing something similar all along.

A designer encounters a choice.

If they understand the mechanisms and consequences surrounding that choice, they have greater capacity to act intentionally.

If they don’t, the choice can become automatic.

Inherited.

Unexamined.

Aesthetic preference masquerading as reasoning.

Software default masquerading as intention.

Convention masquerading as necessity.

AI output masquerading as authorship.

The designer may still be making the decision.

But how much agency is actually present in that decision?

A possible relationship

Perhaps there is a relationship between:

Understanding

→

Awareness

→

Choice

→

Agency

→

Responsibility

Understanding gives us awareness of what is happening.

Awareness gives us the ability to recognize possibilities.

Possibilities make meaningful choice possible.

Meaningful choice creates agency.

And agency creates the possibility of responsibility.

If that relationship holds, then design literacy isn’t simply the ability to make something.

It isn’t even simply the ability to explain something.

It may be the ability to understand and account for the choices through which something came to exist.

The designer’s decisions don’t stop at the artifact

Consider a button.

Why is it there?

Why is it there?

Why is it there?

The first answer might be visual.

The second might be functional.

The third might be systemic.

Where the button sits affects what people notice.

What people notice affects what they do.

What they do affects the system.

The same thing happens with type, images, datasets, categories, maps, interfaces, algorithms, defaults, and interactions.

A designer’s decisions participate in larger systems.

So perhaps asking a designer to account for those decisions isn’t merely an exercise in professional communication.

Perhaps it is an exercise in responsible participation.

But there is a problem

I don’t want to make the argument too simple.

Being able to articulate a decision does not necessarily mean the decision is good.

A designer can rationalize almost anything.

A confident explanation isn’t evidence of understanding.

And not being able to articulate something perfectly doesn’t mean a designer has no understanding.

Design knowledge can be tacit.

Intuition matters.

Experience matters.

Making matters.

Sometimes we know something before we know how to explain it.

So perhaps the goal isn’t:

Can you explain every decision?

Maybe the better question is:

Can you meaningfully account for the decisions you make?

That leaves room for uncertainty.

It leaves room for intuition.

It leaves room for experimentation.

It leaves room for being wrong.

And it leaves room for changing your mind.

This changes how I think about critique

Maybe critique isn’t fundamentally about defending the work.

Maybe it is about making decisions available for examination.

A critique creates a temporary situation in which a designer’s decisions become visible.

Not just the final artifact.

The thinking behind it.

The assumptions.

The alternatives.

The evidence.

The uncertainties.

The consequences.

The things the designer didn’t notice.

And then something important can happen.

The designer can learn something they didn’t know when they made the decision.

Which means critique isn’t simply a test of whether the designer can defend the work.

It can become a mechanism through which the designer’s agency develops.

You make a decision.

You expose the decision.

Someone questions it.

You reconsider it.

You learn.

You make another decision.

That is not just presentation.

That is practice.

And then there is AI

This question becomes particularly strange when the designer isn’t the only thing making decisions.

A generative system can produce an image.

A recommendation system can determine what someone sees.

A template can establish hierarchy.

A software default can determine behavior.

A dataset can establish what counts as a category.

An AI system can introduce possibilities the designer did not explicitly construct.

So who is defending the decision?

The designer?

The system?

The person who selected the output?

The person who built the model?

The person who chose the dataset?

Perhaps this is one reason the question feels increasingly urgent.

Technology gives us more and more ability to produce things without necessarily increasing our understanding of how those things came to be.

And if agency requires some degree of understanding, then technological literacy becomes increasingly important to design practice.

Not because designers need to control every system.

But because they need to understand enough to know where their decisions actually exist.

A question I can’t quite answer yet

Maybe this is why we defend design.

Not because every design decision needs a clever explanation.

Not because clients demand confidence.

Not because professors need something to grade.

But because design is an act of intervention.

We change something.

We organize something.

We communicate something.

We construct something.

We influence something.

And when we do that, we make choices.

Perhaps asking a designer to account for those choices is one small way of asking:

Did you understand what you were doing?

And perhaps the deeper question is:

Could you have acted differently?

If the answer is yes, then there was a choice.

If there was a choice, there was agency.

And if there was agency, perhaps there is responsibility.

I don’t know yet if that chain holds.

But I think it is worth following.

The question

Is the ability to meaningfully account for one’s design decisions a form of design literacy?

And if it is:

Does design literacy create a greater capacity for agency and responsible action?

And if so:

Should critique and design education be understood not merely as teaching students to defend their work, but as teaching them to understand, question, and take responsibility for the decisions through which their work comes into being?

I don’t have the answer yet.

That’s probably why I’m writing this down.

Still figuring it out.
