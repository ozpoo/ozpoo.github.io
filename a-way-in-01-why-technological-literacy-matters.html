A Way In — 01: Why Technological Literacy Matters

A Way In: A series of public essays about what it means to live in a technological world — and what it might mean to become literate within it.

Why am I here today?

The answer to that question is very important to me.

But it’s also been very hard for me to explain.

So, if you would,

I’d like to ask you to join me in a story.

I’d like you to imagine someone in your life.

Someone dear to you.

A parent.

A partner.

A friend.

Someone you’ve connected with through your community.

Someone you love.

And this person is special.

You, your friends, their loved ones, their friends—

everyone who knows them thinks they’re smart.

Capable.

Caring.

Helpful.

They give life meaning.

They make things better.

And now imagine that this person doesn’t know how to read or write.

And somewhere in the back of your mind,

you can’t help but wonder:

What if someone had given them the opportunity?

The resources.

The encouragement.

The support.

The space.

What if someone had simply taken the time to teach them how to read and write?

How might that have changed their life?

Their work?

The things they loved?

Their relationships?

Their ability to understand the world around them?

And what might that have meant for all of the people whose lives they would eventually touch?

I’d like you to hold onto that feeling.

That question.

That perspective.

Because I’d now like to invite you into a similar story.

What if I told you that, from the vantage point of my own life,

every day I interact with people like this?

Loved ones.

Family members.

Students.

Peers.

People who come to me with their fears and questions about AI and technology.

Or maybe someone I pass on the street who just needs a little help getting their credit card reader to work.

And what I see are the same things.

Smart people.

Capable people.

Caring people.

Creative people.

People who are trying to understand the world

and make their way through it.

And I can’t help but wonder:

What if someone gave them the time?

The permission.

The space.

The encouragement.

The resources.

The validation.

What if someone helped them learn how these technologies work?

What might that change for them?

For their lives?

For the people they love?

For the things they create?

For the communities around them?

What could that mean?

That’s why I’m here today.

Because just as we have collectively witnessed and agreed upon the meaningful impact literacy can have on a person’s life—

and on the lives of the people around them—

I believe something similar is true of technological literacy.

I’m not here because I think I’m somehow different from you.

I’m just a dude.

I’m just as human as any of you.

But my path through life has given me a particular kind of literacy.

I’ve had opportunities to learn how technologies work.

I’ve had people who showed me things.

I’ve had permission to experiment.

I’ve had the space to break things.

I’ve had the privilege of asking,

“Why does it work that way?”

And I’ve experienced, firsthand,

what that literacy can give a person.

So I’m not here to tell you that technology is good.

Or bad.

Right or wrong.

Necessary or unnecessary.

And I’m certainly not here to tell you that everyone needs to understand every technology.

That’s impossible.

We don’t learn to read because we’re expected to read every book.

That’s impossible.

We learn to read so that when we encounter a book,

we have the ability to engage with it.

We can open it.

We can understand it.

We can question it.

We can decide whether we want to keep reading.

We can put it down.

We can pick up another one.

And maybe,

eventually,

we can write one ourselves.

That’s what I mean when I talk about technological literacy.

I’m not saying everyone needs to know how every technology works.

I’m saying people should have the ability to encounter a technology

and begin to figure it out.

What is it?

What does it do?

How does it work?

Who made it?

Who controls it?

What choices were made?

What choices do I have?

What happens if it fails?

Can I change it?

Can I refuse it?

Can I imagine something different?

Because increasingly,

people don’t even get the opportunity to ask those questions.

Technology arrives already packaged.

Already designed.

Already embedded in the things we use.

Sometimes we don’t get to choose whether we encounter it.

We don’t get to choose whether we’re aware of it.

We don’t get to choose whether we understand it.

And sometimes we aren’t even told that there are choices.

And without literacy,

you don’t even have the opportunity to learn,

question,

discuss,

understand,

participate,

or choose

what a technology can—

or cannot—

mean in your life.

And this brings me to the conversation that has brought so many of us into this room:

AI.

Because I think we’re sometimes trying to answer questions about AI

before we’ve figured out what we’re actually talking about.

So I’d like to try another thought experiment.

What if I told you that trying to answer the question:

“What is AI?”

is actually more similar to trying to answer:

“What is electricity?”

than it is to answering:

“What is a car?”

Think about that for a second.

A car is a thing.

We can point at it.

We can describe its parts.

We can talk about how those parts work together.

We can ask who made it,

how much it costs,

how fast it goes.

But electricity?

That’s different.

Electricity isn’t one thing in that same sense.

It’s a phenomenon.

A material condition.

A means.

A utility.

Something that can be generated,

stored,

transmitted,

controlled,

consumed,

and used to make other things happen.

And suddenly a question like:

“Is electricity hurting creativity?”

sounds a little strange.

So I’d like you to take a minute.

If someone asked you:

“Is electricity hurting creativity?”

What would your answer be?

How would you even begin?

What kinds of outcomes could possibly come from answering that question?

I don’t know about you,

but when I try to answer it, my first reaction is:

Well... what do you mean?

What kind?

Where?

How is it generated?

How is it being used?

What is it powering?

Who controls it?

Who benefits?

What are the consequences?

What choices were made?

The question is just too big.

Because electricity isn’t simply a thing.

It’s a means through which other things happen.

And so if some of our questions about AI are operating at that same level...

Well, shit.

Maybe we should figure out what we’re actually talking about first.

What kind?

Who owns it?

How was it trained?

How is it implemented?

Where does it operate?

What data does it use?

What does it control?

Who benefits?

What incentives are involved?

How is it being used?

Is it necessary?

What happens when it fails?

And suddenly:

“What is AI?”

starts to feel almost impossible.

It’s everywhere.

It’s increasingly invisible.

It’s a product.

It’s an algorithm.

It’s a service.

It’s a feature.

It’s a recommendation.

It’s a button.

It’s a model.

It’s a function inside another piece of software.

Hell—

it’s a function on my toaster.

Wait.

Why is there AI in my toaster?

Do I need AI in my toaster?

What happens if it breaks?

And believe it or not,

there was a time when you could buy a toaster

and it came with something remarkable.

Paper.

Maybe a booklet.

Maybe just a few sheets.

But it was a manual.

And if you knew how to read,

you could take that manual out of the box

and it would tell you what the buttons did.

What the knobs controlled.

What to expect.

There might even be a diagram.

It would show you the parts.

How they connected.

How the thing functioned.

Maybe it would tell you what part you needed when something broke.

Your literacy gave you a relationship with that toaster.

You didn’t know how to use it because you had somehow been born knowing how that particular toaster worked.

You knew how to use it because someone had given you a way to learn it.

You had a manual.

And that’s the distinction I’m trying to make.

When you bought that toaster,

you had never used that toaster before.

You had never seen that model before.

You didn’t know its particular buttons or mechanisms.

But you could read the manual.

You could learn.

You could understand.

You could operate it.

Maybe you could even take it apart.

You had a pathway into the technology.

Now imagine the toaster doesn’t come with a manual.

Instead,

it has AI.

You’ve never encountered that particular system before.

You don’t know what it’s doing.

You don’t know how it was trained.

You don’t know what information it uses.

You don’t know what decisions it makes.

You don’t know how much energy it consumes.

You don’t know who designed it.

You don’t know who benefits from it.

And if it breaks,

maybe you don’t even know what “breaks” means anymore.

But technological literacy might give you a way in.

It could allow you to start asking:

What does this AI actually control?

What information does it use?

How was it trained?

Has it been tested?

Who made it?

Who benefits from it?

What happens if it fails?

Is there a fallback?

Can I turn it off?

Can I change it?

And maybe the most important question of all:

Do I even need an AI toaster?

And that’s what I mean by literacy.

Not mastery.

Not expertise.

Not knowing everything.

Agency.

The ability to encounter something you don’t understand

and believe:

“I can learn this.”

Because that, to me, is the real gift of literacy.

It isn’t that the world suddenly becomes simple.

It isn’t that every book becomes readable.

It isn’t that every technology becomes understandable.

It’s that you are no longer completely dependent on someone else to tell you what the thing means.

You have a way in.

You have questions.

You have choices.

You can participate.

You can disagree.

You can refuse.

You can build.

You can make something different.

And maybe that’s why I’m here.

Not to convince you that technology is good.

Not to convince you that AI is good.

Not to convince you that everyone needs to become a technologist.

I’m here because I believe

more people deserve a way in.

I believe more people deserve the opportunity to become technologically literate.

Because the technologies shaping our lives are becoming too important

to remain things that simply happen to us.

And I don’t think the answer is for everyone to learn everything.

That’s impossible.

The answer is to give people enough literacy that,

when they encounter something new,

they aren’t forced to simply accept it as magic.

They can ask:

“What is this?”

“How does it work?”

“Why was it made this way?”

“Who decided?”

“What are my choices?”

“What could it be instead?”

And maybe—

just maybe—

“Can I make something different?”

Because I think that’s what literacy has always given us.

Not all the answers.

Just the ability to ask better questions.

And in a world increasingly shaped by technologies we didn’t build,

don’t fully understand,

and didn’t necessarily choose...

I think giving people that ability might be one of the most important things we can do.

That’s why I’m here today.

Author’s Note

I keep coming back to the image of a person who cannot read.

Not because I think technological literacy is the same thing as learning to read.

It isn’t.

But because I keep thinking about what literacy gives us.

Not just information.

Not just a skill.

A relationship with the world.

The ability to encounter something unfamiliar and believe there might be a way into it.

I’ve been lucky enough to have people give me that.

Teachers.

Friends.

Colleagues.

People who let me ask stupid questions.

People who let me break things.

People who showed me that the thing I didn’t understand wasn’t necessarily beyond me.

And I think there are people all around us who deserve that same opportunity.

People who are already smart.

Already creative.

Already capable.

They don’t need to become someone else.

They just need a way in.

That’s what I’m trying to figure out how to give them.

— Oz

Still figuring it out.
