The Foundations

Technology & Literacy Frameworks

An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

Primary researcher: Eric Andren

The Question

How can humans remain capable of understanding, questioning, and shaping the technological systems through which they increasingly live?

The work began with an attempt to understand AI.

It has become an inquiry into something larger:

Technology, literacy, learning, agency, responsibility, and the relationship between people and the systems they create and inhabit.

AI is no longer the destination.

It is a cultural lens and case study through which the larger question became visible.

The technology changed.

The underlying question did not.

The Thesis

People need more than access to technology.

They need the literacy and agency to understand, question, and participate responsibly in the technological systems that shape their lives.

The goal is simple:

Help people understand the systems that shape their lives well enough to participate in shaping them.

Or, more simply:

Agency through understanding.

The Inquiry

The Foundations asks what technological literacy actually is, how it develops, and whether it can be taught through a generalizable approach.

It investigates:

  • How technology can be understood as a system, rather than simply an artifact or tool.
  • How systems encode values, assumptions, incentives, constraints, defaults, and purposes.
  • How people move from awareness → understanding → agency → responsibility.
  • Whether taking systems apart can provide a transferable method for understanding them.
  • What allows understanding to become meaningful participation.
  • What technological literacy might mean beyond professional competence.

This raises a larger possibility:

Technological literacy may not primarily be a professional competency. It may increasingly be a civic competency.

The Framework

Technology is understood as a system of:

Structure → Translation → Behavior → Constraints → Defaults → Purpose

Technological literacy is therefore defined as:

The capacity to encounter, investigate, evaluate, and participate in technological systems with understanding, agency, and responsibility.

The larger recursive model is:

Values → Technology → System → Consequences → Awareness → Literacy → Agency → Responsibility → Participation → System Change → Values

The cycle is never finished.

Systems shape people.

People shape systems.

The question is how much of that relationship we are capable of seeing—and therefore how much of it we are capable of shaping.

The Method

Take It Apart

A way of approaching technology—and, ultimately, the unknown.

The method begins with a simple shift:

I don’t understand this yet.
Let’s take it apart.

Not: I understand technology.

Not: I am technical.

Just: I don’t understand this yet.

The general practice is:

Define → Decompose → Identify → Connect → Trace → Expose → Constrain → Question → Experiment → Model → Reconstruct

  • Take something apart.
  • Understand its parts.
  • Understand their relationships.
  • Trace what is being translated.
  • Expose assumptions and defaults.
  • Identify what the system enables, prevents, or makes difficult.
  • Ask why it is this way.
  • Change something.
  • Observe what happens.
  • Build a model that allows you to predict what happens next.
  • Then reconstruct it—or build something different.

The process is recursive.

  • We move backward.
  • We discover something.
  • We revise the model.
  • We take another piece apart.
  • We try again.

Understanding emerges through repetition.

The method is not ultimately about objects.

It is not even really about technology.

It is a method for approaching the unknown.

Deconstruction as a form of agency.

The Lineage

The idea of taking things apart to understand them is not new.

There is an established tradition across technology and engineering education, reverse engineering, computational thinking, constructionism, maker education, systems thinking, and design education of using decomposition, dissection, reconstruction, and experimentation as ways of learning.

That is good news.

The contribution is not:

I invented taking things apart to learn.

The more interesting question is:

What happens when we recognize these practices as manifestations of a broader literacy practice—and deliberately connect deconstruction, organization, reconstruction, experimentation, agency, and responsibility?

That turns a familiar pedagogical technique into a researchable proposition:

Can taking things apart become a general method for developing technological literacy?

And, ultimately:

What happens when we teach people not simply how to use technological systems, but how to figure out unfamiliar ones?

The Educational Model

Technological literacy is not binary.

It is not literate / illiterate.

It is a multidimensional relationship with a system that can become increasingly meaningful:

Encounter → Operate → Understand → Question → Navigate → Modify → Create → Teach → Transform

These are not necessarily stages.

  • A person may understand one dimension of a system deeply while remaining inexperienced in another.
  • Someone may know how to operate a technology without understanding how it works.
  • Someone may understand how it works without questioning its assumptions.
  • Someone may recognize its consequences without knowing how to intervene.

Literacy therefore involves more than knowledge.

It involves the increasing capacity to see, understand, question, and act.

A related educational progression is:

Awareness → Knowledge → Literacy → Agency → Responsibility

The Larger Pedagogical Principle

The work is less interested in teaching people what technology is than in developing their capacity to figure out what something is.

The aim is not technological mastery.

It is transferable understanding.

If the method works, encountering something unfamiliar should become less threatening.

The question changes from:

What am I supposed to know about this?

to:

What can I figure out about this?

That distinction may be one of the most important ideas in the entire project.

The Research Practice

The work does not begin with a fully formed research agenda.

It begins with observation.

Experience → Question → Wandering → Pattern → Model → Testing → Framework → Public Invitation → Scholarship

The practice is to notice something, follow it, look for patterns, construct provisional models, test them, and make them available for others to challenge.

The framework is therefore not meant to be a finished answer.

It is a thing that should change through use.

The work does not claim to be the only way.

It does not claim to be the best way.

It does not claim to have invented the methods it draws upon.

It is an attempt to make a useful way of seeing—and to continually question that way of seeing.

The Applications

The ideas are being tested through research, curriculum, computational tools, and teaching:

  • ISU Catalog AI — a preliminary computational exploration of how artificial intelligence appears in the Iowa State University curriculum.
  • Catalog AI — an open computational tool for exploring large course catalogs through deliberately limited vocabularies.
  • The Foundations Series — working frameworks for thinking about technology, technological literacy, learning, agency, and responsibility.
  • Design Technology I + II — a technological foundations sequence for graphic design that treats technology as an active design medium rather than simply a collection of software tools.
  • AI for Design and Visual Thinking — curriculum exploring the intersection of visual communication, design thinking, and emerging technologies.
  • Assessing AI Readiness — research examining how graphic design faculty and students are currently engaging with artificial intelligence.
  • Computational tools, student activities, and curriculum analysis — smaller experiments through which the framework is tested in practice.

These are not separate projects so much as different places to test the same questions.

The Public Work

The research is also being developed in public.

A Way In

Essays about what it means to live in a technological world—and what it might mean to become literate within it.

Field Notes

Working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.

AI for Design Thinking

Small experiments exploring AI as something a designer can think with during the design process.

Alongside these:

Talks · Conference Proposals · Exhibitions · Interactive Experiments · Teaching Materials

The public work is not simply dissemination.

It is another form of testing.

Put the idea somewhere people can encounter it. See what happens.

The Questions Still Open

The work is intentionally unfinished.

Technology

  • What is technology beyond software and artifacts?
  • How do technological systems encode values, assumptions, incentives, and constraints?
  • Why do systems produce consequences their creators or users did not intend?

Literacy

  • What does it actually mean to be technologically literate?
  • Can technological literacy be developed through a generalizable method?
  • What separates awareness from understanding?
  • What separates understanding from agency?

Learning

  • Why does taking things apart seem to produce understanding?
  • Is reconstruction a meaningful mechanism of learning?
  • Can the method transfer across disciplines?

Agency

  • Why does understanding not always lead to action?
  • What prevents people from intervening in systems they recognize as problematic?
  • Under what conditions can individuals meaningfully shape technological systems?

Responsibility

  • What responsibilities emerge once we understand the systems we participate in?
  • How should technological literacy relate to ethics?
  • Can education cultivate not only technological capability, but technological responsibility?

Design

  • What should designers understand about the systems they create?
  • Can design education move beyond software proficiency toward technological agency?

The Observations

Several ideas are beginning to emerge.

Literacy might be the ability to recognize patterns, construct models from them, and act through those models.

Literacy might be the ability to move between models, scales, relationships, and perspectives—not simply to possess information.

Literacy might be the ability to recognize what is a pattern, what is an instance, what rules produce the pattern, and where the instance can escape the model.

These are not conclusions.

They are working observations.

The Measure

Success is not primarily recognition.

It is usefulness.

  • Does the work help someone encounter an unfamiliar technology and feel capable of understanding it?
  • Does a student realize they are not bad at technology—they simply have not yet been given a way in?
  • Does a designer begin questioning the defaults embedded in the systems they create?
  • Does an educator find a useful way to teach technological understanding?
  • Does a researcher challenge the framework and make it stronger?
  • Does the framework change through use?

And ultimately:

Does this work help people become more capable participants in the technological systems that shape their lives?

The Destination

Unknown.

And that is okay.

The work is not trying to arrive at a final definition of technological literacy.

It is trying to develop a way of seeing that remains useful as technology changes.

Because the technologies will continue to change.

The systems will change.

The questions will change.

The framework should change with them.

What matters is preserving the capacity to ask:

I don’t understand this yet.
Let’s take it apart.

And then:

Understand it. Question it. Test it. Rebuild it.

Participate.

Still figuring it out.
