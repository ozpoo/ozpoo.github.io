AI for Design Thinking
Practices for using AI within the design process.

Introduction

AI as a Design Thinking Practice

AI systems can be used in many different ways during a design process.

They can generate possibilities, respond to questions, transform information, produce alternatives, simulate perspectives, compare material, and interact with ideas through language or other forms of input and output.

These capabilities can be approached from many directions.

This collection focuses on one particular use case:

Using AI as a participant in design thinking.

Rather than treating AI as a single-purpose tool, these practices explore different roles it can take within a design process.

The role changes the interaction.

  • As a brainstorm partner, AI can be used to generate possibilities.
  • As a critique partner, it can be asked to examine a proposal.
  • As a research partner, it can help identify questions and connections to investigate.
  • As a simulator, it can be used to explore how a concept might behave in a particular situation.

The purpose of these practices is not to prescribe what AI should or should not be used for.

They provide different ways to frame an interaction with an AI system around a particular design-thinking activity.

A Focused Use Case

Design thinking involves many kinds of activity:

  • Observing
  • Questioning
  • Researching
  • Generating
  • Comparing
  • Interpreting
  • Testing
  • Reflecting
  • Communicating
  • Deciding

AI can be introduced into different parts of that process.

These practices focus specifically on interactions where AI is used to participate in one of those activities.

The same AI system can therefore be approached in different ways depending on the question being asked.

The distinction is not necessarily about what the technology is.

It is about how the interaction is framed.

Working With the Practices

Each practice begins with a particular role and question.

  • The role provides a frame for the interaction.
  • The question provides a starting point.
  • The resulting exchange is material to examine.

A useful response may generate a new direction, expose an assumption, suggest a connection, provide an alternative interpretation, or simply give you something to respond to.

It may also be inaccurate, incomplete, repetitive, or unrelated to the problem.

The practices therefore emphasize interaction and examination, rather than treating an AI response as a predetermined outcome.

  • Try a role.
  • Observe what happens.
  • Change the question.
  • Change the role.
  • Compare the results.

Decide what, if anything, the interaction contributes to the design process.

Choosing a Practice

Different design activities call for different kinds of interaction.

If you want to… Try…
Generate possibilities Brainstorm Partner
Examine an idea Critique Partner
Explore an opposing position Devil’s Advocate
Reframe a problem Metaphor Machine
Identify recurring ideas Pattern Finder
Examine your process Reflection Partner
Identify questions and connections Research Partner
Explore possible situations Simulator
Test an idea across language or form Translator
Rehearse an interaction User Tester
Explore different perspectives Audience

These are not mutually exclusive.

A project may move between several practices as the design process develops.

The Role Is the Method

The central idea behind these practices is simple:

Change the role, and you change the interaction.

“Give me ideas” produces one kind of exchange.
“Challenge this assumption” produces another.
“Act as a first-time user” produces another.
“Find patterns across these observations” produces another.

The purpose of the framework is to make those differences explicit.

Instead of treating AI as a general-purpose interaction, the designer can define what kind of interaction is needed and structure the exchange accordingly.

Combining Practices

The practices do not need to be used independently.

A design process may move through several roles as the question changes.

For example:

Brainstorm Partner → Critique Partner → Devil’s Advocate → Reflection Partner

Generate possibilities.
Examine them.
Challenge the assumptions behind them.
Look back at what changed.

Or:

Research Partner → Pattern Finder → Metaphor Machine → Simulator

Identify questions.
Look for recurring ideas.
Reframe what you found.
Explore how a concept might behave in a particular situation.

The sequence is not fixed.

A designer might move from one role to another, return to an earlier role, or combine several roles around the same problem.

The point is not to follow a prescribed workflow.

It is to make the kind of interaction explicit as the design process changes.

Change the question. Change the role. Change the interaction.

A Note on Evaluation

An AI interaction is not automatically useful simply because it produces an interesting response.

Likewise, an unexpected or incorrect response may still become useful material for further investigation.

Evaluate the interaction in relation to the design activity:

  • Did it produce useful possibilities?
  • Did it reveal a question?
  • Did it surface an assumption?
  • Did it provide a perspective worth investigating?
  • Did it expose something that needs to be tested?
  • Did it change how you understood the problem?

The value of the interaction depends on what it contributes to the particular design process.
