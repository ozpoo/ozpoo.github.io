The Conditions for Agency

From understanding technology to taking responsibility for what we do with it.

What do we do with what we now understand?

Status: Working framework
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

A part of—The Foundations:
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

THE FINAL QUESTION

We began this series with a simple impulse:

Let’s take it apart.

That impulse led somewhere larger than expected.

We began by asking what technology actually is.

We developed a broader understanding of technology as a system of structure, translation, behavior, constraints, defaults, and purpose.

We considered what it means to become literate in those systems.

We developed a method for taking things apart in order to understand them.

We explored reconstruction as a way to transform understanding into capability.

We looked outward and discovered that technology does not exist independently from culture.

It is made by people.

It reflects values.

It shapes behavior.

It participates in the systems that shape our lives.

And eventually, the questions became unavoidable:

If we understand these systems, what can we do?

And:

What responsibility comes with being able to do it?

This is where literacy becomes agency.

And where agency becomes responsibility.

LITERACY

Literacy is often understood as the ability to read and write.

But literacy has always been larger than that.

To be literate in something is to develop enough understanding to meaningfully participate in it.

Technological literacy therefore cannot simply mean knowing how to operate software.

It means developing the ability to:

  • identify systems,
  • understand their components,
  • recognize relationships,
  • interpret behavior,
  • identify constraints,
  • recognize defaults,
  • question assumptions,
  • understand purposes,
  • evaluate consequences,
  • and participate intentionally.

Literacy is not mastery.

It is not knowing everything.

It is not memorizing every tool.

It is not being the smartest person in the room.

It is having enough understanding to ask better questions and make informed choices.

LITERACY AS ORIENTATION

Perhaps literacy is best understood as orientation.

An unfamiliar system can feel like a black box.

We encounter it from the outside.

We know what it does.

We may know how to operate it.

But we do not necessarily know how to understand it.

Literacy changes our position.

We begin to see:

This connects to that.

This behavior comes from this structure.

This limitation is intentional.

This default reflects a decision.

This outcome is an incentive.

This feature exists because someone decided it should.

The system becomes navigable.

We are no longer merely inside it.

We can begin to see where we are.

FROM LITERACY TO AGENCY

Understanding creates possibilities.

If I do not understand a system, my choices are constrained by what the system presents to me.

If I understand the system, I can begin to recognize alternatives.

This is agency.

Agency does not mean unlimited freedom.

It means recognizing and exercising the choices available within a system.

A literate person can ask:

Do I have to do it this way?

What happens if I don’t?

What else could I use?

Can this be changed?

Who decided this was the default?

Can I build something different?

Those questions are small acts of agency.

AGENCY IS NOT CONTROL

This distinction matters.

We often talk about agency as though it means control.

But complex systems rarely give individuals complete control.

We cannot individually redesign an economy.

We cannot personally rebuild the internet.

We cannot change every platform policy.

We cannot eliminate every environmental cost.

We cannot control every consequence of a technology.

Agency is therefore not:

I control the system.

It is:

I understand enough of the system to recognize where I can act.

Sometimes that action is changing something.

Sometimes it is refusing something.

Sometimes it is adapting.

Sometimes it is organizing with others.

Sometimes it is designing an alternative.

Sometimes it is simply asking a question that would otherwise go unasked.

Agency exists within constraints.

Understanding helps us see those constraints.

FROM AGENCY TO RESPONSIBILITY

And then comes the uncomfortable part.

If I understand that my actions have consequences, what do I do with that knowledge?

This is where responsibility enters.

Responsibility is not punishment.

It is not guilt.

It is not perfection.

It is the recognition that our choices participate in systems larger than ourselves.

Once we understand those systems, we can no longer pretend that our choices exist in isolation.

We become participants.

And participation carries responsibility.

RESPONSIBILITY IS NOT THE SAME AS BLAME

This distinction is particularly important when discussing technology.

Complex technological systems rarely have a single cause.

An outcome may involve:

developers,

designers,

executives,

investors,

institutions,

governments,

users,

markets,

infrastructure,

cultural expectations,

and historical conditions.

Assigning blame to one component may feel satisfying while doing little to explain the system.

Responsibility asks a different question:

Who has the capacity to influence what happens next?

That question can be distributed.

Different participants have different forms and degrees of responsibility.

A user.

A designer.

A teacher.

A company.

A university.

A government.

A community.

A society.

Responsibility follows agency.

Where we have meaningful ability to act, we have some responsibility for how we act.

RESPONSIBILITY REQUIRES CONTEXT

There is no universal technological morality that can tell us what to do in every situation.

The same technology can produce different consequences in different contexts.

A tool that increases accessibility for one person may reduce autonomy for another.

Automation that removes dangerous labor may also eliminate livelihoods.

A generative system that enables expression may undermine the economic conditions of the people whose work trained it.

A highly efficient system may become dangerously fragile.

A slower system may be more resilient.

There are rarely simple answers.

Responsibility therefore requires contextual judgment.

We have to understand the system before we can responsibly intervene in it.

RESPONSIBILITY REQUIRES QUESTIONS

Responsible participation begins with inquiry.

Before adopting a technology:

What problem are we actually trying to solve?

Why is this technology appropriate?

What alternatives exist?

What does it require?

What does it consume?

What does it replace?

What does it enable?

What does it constrain?

Who benefits?

Who bears the cost?

What behaviors does it encourage?

What assumptions does it embed?

What happens if we are wrong?

These questions do not guarantee good decisions.

They make better decisions possible.

RESPONSIBILITY AND PURPOSE

This returns us to the final element of the technology framework:

Purpose.

A technology without a purpose is simply capability.

The meaningful question is what that capability is directed toward.

This is especially important because technological development can create a strange inversion:

We develop a capability and then search for reasons to use it.

The existence of a technology becomes its own justification.

We can do this.

Therefore we should.

But capability does not establish necessity.

The better question is:

What is this capability in service of?

And:

Is that purpose worth the costs required to achieve it?

NECESSITY

This is where the original AI project returns.

AI is extraordinarily capable.

That capability is fascinating.

It can be useful.

It can be beautiful.

It can enable things that were previously difficult or impossible.

But capability creates a temptation:

If we can do it, why wouldn’t we?

The answer may simply be:

Because we don’t need to.

Not every possible technology needs to be deployed everywhere.

Not every task needs to be automated.

Not every process needs to be optimized.

Not every inefficiency is a problem.

Sometimes the most responsible technological decision is not to build something.

Or not to use it.

Or to use a smaller system.

Or to use it less.

Or to use it differently.

Technological literacy gives us the ability to ask that question.

Agency gives us the ability to act on the answer.

Responsibility asks us to consider whether we should.

THE RIGHT TO SAY NO

This may be one of the most important forms of technological agency.

We are often taught to think of technological progress as inevitable.

A new technology appears.

Adoption follows.

Resistance becomes temporary.

Eventually everyone uses it.

But inevitability is often a story we tell after decisions have already been made.

People can choose.

Organizations can choose.

Institutions can choose.

Communities can choose.

Societies can choose.

Sometimes the responsible decision is:

No.

Not because the technology is evil.

Not because progress is bad.

But because the particular implementation, purpose, timing, cost, or context is wrong.

A literate person should be capable of saying yes.

They should also be capable of saying no.

RESPONSIBILITY AND DESIGN

Designers have a particularly important relationship to this progression.

Design creates possibilities.

It also creates constraints.

Every interface has defaults.

Every workflow establishes behavior.

Every system prioritizes something.

Every decision excludes something else.

Design therefore participates in agency.

We can make systems easier to understand.

We can expose choices.

We can hide them.

We can encourage reflection.

We can encourage speed.

We can make consequences visible.

We can make them invisible.

This means design is not simply about making desirable experiences.

It is also about deciding what kinds of behavior those experiences make possible.

DESIGNING FOR AGENCY

If literacy helps people understand systems, design can either support or undermine that literacy.

A system designed for agency might:

  • make important information visible,
  • explain meaningful consequences,
  • expose rather than conceal defaults,
  • provide genuine choices,
  • allow users to recover from mistakes,
  • make systems understandable,
  • support experimentation,
  • preserve user control,
  • and avoid unnecessary dependency.

This is not always the easiest design.

It may not be the fastest.

It may not maximize engagement.

It may not maximize conversion.

It may not maximize profit.

But it may maximize something else:

the user’s ability to participate meaningfully.

EDUCATION AND THE RESPONSIBILITY OF LITERACY

This progression also changes how education can be understood.

If education simply transfers information, literacy may be the endpoint.

But if education is intended to prepare people to participate in the world, literacy is only the beginning.

A student should ideally leave education able to:

understand → question → act → evaluate → revise

This is not merely technical competence.

It is citizenship.

It is professional practice.

It is creative practice.

It is participation in culture.

The responsibility of education is therefore not simply to prepare people to use the systems that already exist.

It can also prepare them to question, reshape, and build the systems that come next.

RESPONSIBILITY IS COLLECTIVE

There is a danger in placing all technological responsibility on individuals.

An individual cannot reasonably be expected to solve structural problems alone.

If a system is harmful, telling every user to simply “make better choices” can obscure the organizations and institutions that created the conditions in which those choices occur.

Responsibility must therefore exist at multiple levels.

INDIVIDUAL

How do I participate?

PROFESSIONAL

How do I use my expertise?

ORGANIZATIONAL

What systems do we create and maintain?

INSTITUTIONAL

What do our policies and structures encourage?

SOCIETAL

What do we collectively choose to normalize?

No single level is sufficient.

The systems are interconnected.

So is responsibility.

RESPONSIBILITY AS CONTINUOUS PRACTICE

Responsibility is not a final decision.

Technology changes.

Knowledge changes.

Consequences emerge.

New evidence appears.

Systems evolve.

What seemed reasonable yesterday may become questionable tomorrow.

Responsible participation therefore requires continued attention.

We should be willing to revise our conclusions.

To change our minds.

To acknowledge unintended consequences.

To recognize when our assumptions were wrong.

To rebuild.

This brings us back to the reconstruction loop.

We do not simply reconstruct technologies.

We reconstruct our understanding of them.

THE COMPLETE LOOP

The Foundations can now be understood as a larger cycle:

ENCOUNTER
↓
TAKE IT APART
↓
UNDERSTAND
↓
RECONSTRUCT
↓
TEST
↓
ACT
↓
OBSERVE CONSEQUENCES
↓
RECONSIDER
↓
RECONSTRUCT AGAIN

Literacy is not a destination.

It is a practice.

Agency is not a possession.

It is exercised.

Responsibility is not a judgment.

It is an ongoing relationship with consequence.

LITERACY → AGENCY → RESPONSIBILITY

The progression can therefore be stated simply:

LITERACY

I can understand the system.

↓

AGENCY

I can recognize where I have choices and act within the system.

↓

RESPONSIBILITY

I recognize that my choices have consequences, and I am accountable for how I participate.

This is not a hierarchy.

Responsibility does not mean agency is surrendered.

Quite the opposite.

Responsibility is what gives agency meaning.

And literacy is what makes meaningful agency possible.

THE PURPOSE OF LITERACY

Perhaps this is ultimately why technological literacy matters.

Not because everyone needs to become an engineer.

Not because everyone needs to understand every algorithm.

Not because everyone needs to learn to code.

Not because everyone needs to become an expert.

But because people should have some ability to understand the systems that increasingly shape their lives.

They should be able to look at something unfamiliar and say:

Let’s take it apart.

They should be able to investigate rather than simply accept.

They should be able to reconstruct rather than simply consume.

They should be able to recognize alternatives.

They should be able to participate.

They should be able to decide.

And when necessary:

They should be able to say no.

THE LARGER PURPOSE

This began as an attempt to understand AI.

It became an attempt to understand technology.

Then technological literacy.

Then learning.

Then design.

Then culture.

Then ourselves.

The questions became larger because the systems were larger than they first appeared.

Perhaps that is the lesson.

When we encounter something complicated, we should resist the temptation to immediately decide whether it is good or bad.

First:

Take it apart.

Find the pieces.

Find the relationships.

Find the assumptions.

Find the incentives.

Find the constraints.

Find the purpose.

Find the people.

Find the consequences.

Then reconstruct.

Try something.

Test it.

Learn.

And then ask the harder question:

Now that I understand this, what should I do?

That is where literacy becomes agency.

And where agency becomes responsibility.

THE QUESTIONS THAT REMAIN

The framework does not provide final answers.

It provides better questions.

  • What technologies are we building?
  • Why are we building them?
  • What problems are we actually trying to solve?
  • What values are embedded in their design?
  • What behaviors do they encourage?
  • What systems do they depend upon?
  • What do they make possible?
  • What do they make difficult?
  • Who benefits?
  • Who bears the costs?
  • What alternatives exist?
  • What happens when we understand the systems we inhabit?
  • What happens when we don’t?
  • Where do we have agency?
  • What responsibilities accompany that agency?
  • What should we build?
  • What should we change?
  • What should we refuse?
  • And perhaps most importantly:

What kind of world are we willing to participate in constructing?

AFTER THE FOUNDATIONS

The purpose of a foundation is not to become the building.

It is to make the building possible.

These frameworks are therefore not intended to be final definitions.

They are tools.

Ways of seeing.

Ways of asking.

Ways of learning.

Ways of working.

They can be challenged.

They can be revised.

They can be discarded.

They can be reconstructed.

Perhaps that is the most appropriate conclusion.

Because the framework itself should remain subject to the method it proposes.

We should be willing to take this apart, too.

LET’S TAKE IT APART.

The phrase that began this inquiry was almost embarrassingly simple.

Let’s take it apart.

It was never really about destruction.

It was curiosity.

A willingness to open the thing.

To look inside.

To separate the pieces.

To understand what each one does.

To discover how they relate.

To figure out why someone built it this way.

And then, perhaps, to put it back together.

Maybe better.

Maybe differently.

Maybe exactly as it was.

Maybe not at all.

The important thing is that we looked.

We learned.

We became capable of making a choice.

And then we chose what to do with what we learned.

That may be the beginning of technological literacy.

It may also be the beginning of something much larger:

the ability to participate deliberately in the world we are constructing.
