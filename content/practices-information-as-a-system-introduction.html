Information as a System
Investigating information through structure

Introduction

Research as Investigation

We often think of research as finding information.

But finding information is only the beginning—the harder problem is making sense of what we find.

Information rarely arrives as a system.

It comes as fragments:

Facts, observations, records, images, dates, names, measurements, stories, sources, and relationships.

Before we can communicate information clearly, we need to understand how those pieces relate.

This is where research becomes investigation.

To investigate is to look closely, organize what you find, test relationships, notice patterns, and construct explanations.

The goal is not simply to collect more information.

The goal is to develop a way of seeing what is already there.

Information Is Not Yet a Structure

A collection of information is not necessarily an organized system.

Consider a collection of:

  • 50 historical events
  • 100 photographs
  • 30 people
  • 200 survey responses
  • 75 products
  • 40 locations
  • 500 lines of text

Simply having the information does not tell us how it works.

We need to ask questions:

  • What belongs together?
  • What is different?
  • What happened before or after something else?
  • What depends on what?
  • What is connected?
  • What patterns are emerging?
  • What relationships matter?

These questions begin to turn information into structure.

Investigating Information

There is no single correct way to organize information.

Different information reveals different kinds of relationships.

  • A timeline may reveal change.
  • A taxonomy may reveal categories.
  • A network may reveal connections.
  • A comparison may reveal differences.
  • A map may reveal spatial relationships.
  • A sequence may reveal process.

The method you choose changes what you are able to see.

This means that information architecture is not simply the act of arranging information.

It is an act of investigation.

A Way In

These guides provide different ways into an information set.

They are not recipes—they are methods for asking better questions.

  • Try one.
  • See what it reveals.
  • Change methods when necessary.
  • Combine methods when useful.
  • Move things around.
  • Test your assumptions.
  • Let the information challenge your first idea.

The structure is not something you simply discover.

It is something you investigate, test, and propose.

The Investigation Process

A useful investigation often moves through six stages:

Find → Organize → Relate → Structure → Test → Explain

  1. Gather the information.
  2. Make the information visible and workable.
  3. Look for connections, differences, sequences, dependencies, and patterns.
  4. Develop possible ways of organizing what you found.
  5. Challenge the structure.
  6. Develop a clear structure that helps someone else understand the information.

Or:

  1. What do you actually have?
  2. What pieces belong together?
  3. How are these things related?
  4. What structures might explain the information?
  5. Does it hold up? What doesn’t fit?
  6. What does this structure allow us to see?

Choosing a Method

Start with the question you are trying to answer.

If you want to understand… Try…
What belongs together Card Sorting
How things can be categorized Taxonomy
What happened when Timeline
What happens before or after Sequence
What is above, below, inside, or dependent on something else Hierarchy
How things are similar or different Comparison
What is connected to what Network
Where things exist in relation to one another Mapping
What kinds of relationships exist Relationship Diagram

You do not need to choose only one.

A strong investigation may use several.

Combining Methods

Real information rarely fits neatly into one structure.

You may begin with a card sort and discover categories.

→ Those categories may become a taxonomy.
→ A timeline may reveal when the categories emerged.
→ A network may reveal relationships between them.
→ A map may reveal where they occur.
→ A sequence may reveal how people move through the system.

The methods can work together.

For example:

Card Sort → Taxonomy → Network → Timeline → Structure

  • What belongs together?
  • How can those groups be organized?
  • How are the groups connected?
  • How have those relationships changed?
  • Which relationships matter most to the audience?

The goal is not to use every method.

The goal is to find the method—or combination of methods—that helps you understand the information.

Testing the Structure

A structure can look convincing and still be wrong, incomplete, or unhelpful.

Before committing to a structure, test it.

Ask: Does everything have a place?

If not, why?

Are the categories meaningful?

Or are they simply convenient?

Are you using the same logic throughout?

Or are some categories based on one characteristic and others on another?

Does the structure reveal something?

Or does it simply rearrange the information?

Can someone else understand it?

Ask another person to explain the structure back to you.

What does the structure hide?

Every structure makes some relationships visible and others less visible.

This is important.

Choosing a structure is choosing what your audience will be able to see.

From Structure to Design

Once you understand the information, you can begin designing its communication.

This is where information architecture becomes visual design.

The process is:

Information → Investigation → Relationships → Structure → Architecture → Visual form

The visual design should not have to invent the structure.

The structure should give the visual design something to work with.
