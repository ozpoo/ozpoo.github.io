Information as a System
Investigating information through structure

Research as Investigation

Research often begins with finding information.

But finding is only the beginning.

Information arrives as fragments: facts, observations, images, records, examples, measurements, stories.

The work is figuring out how those fragments relate.

Information is not yet a structure.

To investigate is to look closely, organize what you find, test relationships, notice patterns, and construct explanations.

The goal is not simply to collect more information.

It is to develop a way of seeing what is already there.

A Way In

These guides are not recipes.

They are methods for asking different questions of information.

Try one.
See what it reveals.
Change the method.
Combine methods.
Move things.
Test your assumptions.

A structure is not something you simply discover.

It is something you investigate, test, and propose.

The Process

Find → Organize → Relate → Structure → Test → Explain

Each step changes what you can see.

Find what is relevant.
Organize what you have collected.
Relate things to one another.
Structure those relationships into a system.
Test whether the system makes sense.
Explain what the structure reveals.

The process is not always linear.

You may discover something while organizing.
A relationship may change the structure.
Testing may send you back to research.

The structure develops through the investigation.

Choosing a Way In

Different methods reveal different kinds of relationships.

Understand how things are categorized Taxonomy
See what happened when Timeline
Understand before and after Sequence
See what sits above, below, inside, or depends on something else Hierarchy
Compare similarities and differences Comparison
See what is connected Network

The method changes what becomes visible.

Combining Methods

A single method rarely tells the whole story.

You might begin with a Card Sort to see what belongs together.

Then build a Taxonomy to describe those groups.

Use a Network to examine relationships between them.

Add a Timeline to understand how those relationships change over time.

Then propose a structure that brings what you discovered together.

The sequence is not fixed.

Move between methods as the question changes.

Change the method. Change what you can see.

Testing the Structure

A structure can look convincing and still be incomplete.

Ask:

  • Does everything have a place?
  • Are the categories meaningful?
  • Is the organizing logic consistent?
  • Does the structure reveal something useful?
  • Can someone else understand it?
  • What does the structure hide?
  • What is missing?

Testing is not the final check before making.

It is another way of investigating.

Choosing a structure is choosing what your audience will be able to see.
