Technology & Literacy Frameworks

The Question

How can humans remain capable of understanding, questioning, and shaping the technological systems through which they increasingly live?

The work began with an attempt to understand AI.

It became an inquiry into something larger:

Technology, literacy, learning, agency, responsibility, and the relationship between people and the systems they create and inhabit.

AI is no longer the destination.

It is a cultural lens and case study through which the larger question became visible.

The technology changed. The underlying question did not.

The Thesis

People need more than access to technology.

They need the literacy and agency to understand, question, and participate responsibly in the technological systems that shape their lives.

Help people understand the systems that shape their lives well enough to participate in shaping them.

Agency through understanding.

This suggests that technological literacy may be more than professional competence. It may increasingly be a civic competency.

The Emerging Framework

Technology is approached not simply as an artifact or tool, but as a system of:

Structure → Translation → Behavior → Constraints → Defaults → Purpose

Technological literacy is therefore understood as:

The capacity to encounter, investigate, evaluate, and participate in technological systems with understanding, agency, and responsibility.

The larger relationship is recursive:

Values → Technology → System → Consequences → Awareness → Literacy → Agency → Responsibility → Participation → System Change → Values

Systems shape people.

People shape systems.

The question is how much of that relationship we are capable of seeing—and therefore how much of it we are capable of shaping.

The Method: Take It Apart

A way of approaching technology—and, ultimately, the unknown.

The method begins with a simple shift:

I don’t understand this yet.
Let’s take it apart.

The practice is:

Define → Decompose → Identify → Connect → Trace → Expose → Constrain → Question → Experiment → Model → Reconstruct

Take something apart. Understand its parts and relationships. Trace what is being translated. Expose assumptions and defaults. Identify what the system enables, prevents, or makes difficult. Change something. Observe what happens. Build a model. Then reconstruct it—or build something different.

The process is recursive.

Understanding emerges through repetition.

The idea of taking things apart is not new. It has established lineages in reverse engineering, technology and engineering education, computational thinking, constructionism, maker education, systems thinking, and design education.

The research question is more specific:

Can these familiar practices be recognized and connected as a broader method for developing technological literacy?

And ultimately:

What happens when we teach people not simply how to use technological systems, but how to figure out unfamiliar ones?

Deconstruction as a form of agency.

The Educational Proposition

The work is less interested in teaching people what technology is than in developing their capacity to figure out what something is.

Technological literacy is not simply knowing more technology. It is an increasing capacity to:

Encounter → Operate → Understand → Question → Navigate → Modify → Create → Teach → Transform

These are not fixed stages. A person may operate a system without understanding it, understand it without questioning it, or recognize its consequences without knowing how to intervene.

The aim is therefore transferable understanding.

The question changes from:

What am I supposed to know about this?

to:

What can I figure out about this?

The Research Program

The framework is being developed through a combination of research, curriculum, computational experimentation, teaching, and public inquiry.

Current applications include:

  • ISU Catalog AI — computational research into how artificial intelligence appears across the Iowa State University curriculum.
  • Design Technology I + II — a technological foundations sequence treating technology as an active design medium rather than simply a collection of software tools.
  • AI for Design and Visual Thinking — curriculum exploring emerging technologies through visual communication and design thinking.
  • Assessing AI Readiness — research examining how graphic design faculty and students are engaging with artificial intelligence.
  • The Foundational Series — working frameworks concerning technology, literacy, learning, agency, and responsibility.
  • Computational tools, student activities, curriculum analysis, talks, conference proposals, and public experiments — smaller sites where the framework is tested through use.

The research is also being developed in public:

  • A Way In — a series of public essays about what it means to live in a technological world—and what it might mean to become literate within it.
  • Field Notes — working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.
  • Working Inquiries — questions and ideas being actively tested before they become public observations, patterns, or frameworks.
  • Practices – practical frameworks and methods for investigating technology, information, and the systems we use to make sense of the world.

These are not separate projects so much as different places to test the same questions.

The research practice itself is:

Experience → Question → Wandering → Pattern → Model → Testing → Framework → Public Invitation → Scholarship

The framework is therefore intentionally provisional.

It is not a finished answer. It is a thing that should change through use.

What Remains Open

The work continues to investigate:

  • What is technology beyond software and artifacts?
  • What does it actually mean to be technologically literate?
  • Why does taking things apart seem to produce understanding?
  • Can the method transfer across disciplines?
  • What separates awareness from understanding—and understanding from agency?
  • Why does understanding not always lead to action?
  • What responsibilities emerge once we understand the systems we participate in?
  • How might design education move beyond software proficiency toward technological agency?

Several working observations are beginning to emerge:

Literacy might be the ability to recognize patterns, construct models from them, and act through those models.

Literacy might be the ability to move between models, scales, relationships, and perspectives—not simply to possess information.

These are not conclusions.

They are working observations being tested through practice.

The Direction

The destination is intentionally unknown.

The goal is not to arrive at a final definition of technological literacy, nor to claim a single method as the answer.

The goal is to develop a way of seeing that remains useful as technology changes.

The technologies will change.

The systems will change.

The questions will change.

The framework should change with them.

What matters is preserving the capacity to ask:

I don’t understand this yet.
Let’s take it apart.

Then:

Understand it. Question it. Test it. Rebuild it.

Participate.
