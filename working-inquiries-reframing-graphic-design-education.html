Reframing Graphic Design Education

If the tools, media, and professional applications of graphic design continue to change, what are the underlying capabilities we believe define a graphic design education?

Status: Early inquiry
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

Part of The Foundations: An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

A proposal for discussion

Introduction

Graphic design is changing.

Its technologies are changing. Its professional contexts are changing. Its relationship to other disciplines is changing. And the scale at which design decisions operate has expanded considerably.

Yet the way graphic design is often described has remained relatively stable.

Graphic design is frequently understood through its visible outputs: logos, publications, posters, advertisements, websites, interfaces, packaging, and other forms of visual communication. These remain important parts of the discipline, but they do not fully describe what contemporary graphic designers are learning to do.

At the same time, practices that have historically developed within or alongside graphic design—user experience, interaction design, information design, creative technology, computational media, service design, and others—have developed increasingly distinct identities and professional vocabularies.

This diversification is not inherently problematic. It reflects the growth of design and the emergence of new technologies and contexts.

However, it raises an important question for graphic design education:

If the tools, media, and professional applications of graphic design continue to change, what are the underlying capabilities we believe define a graphic design education?

This proposal suggests that answering that question should become an explicit part of how we structure, teach, and communicate the discipline.

The problem of defining graphic design by its outputs

One of the challenges facing graphic design education is that the discipline is often easiest to recognize through what students produce rather than what they learn.

A prospective student may see branding, typography, posters, websites, or digital graphics.

A parent may see a creative profession whose applications are difficult to understand.

An employer may see a collection of increasingly specialized roles: brand designer, UX designer, product designer, information designer, motion designer, creative technologist, and others.

Students themselves may initially encounter the discipline through software.

None of these perspectives are incorrect.

But collectively, they can obscure the underlying education.

A contemporary graphic design education is not simply an education in making visual artifacts. It develops the ability to investigate situations, understand relationships, organize information, translate ideas, work across media and technologies, construct systems and experiences, test decisions, and consider the consequences of what is made.

The tools and outputs are expressions of these capabilities.

They are not the capabilities themselves.

A proposed framing

Rather than defining graphic design primarily through its media or outputs, we might define it through the relationships it helps organize.

One possible working definition is:

Graphic design is the practice of organizing information, ideas, technology, and experience so that people can understand and act.

This framing does not attempt to make graphic design synonymous with systems design, UX, AI, or any other emerging field.

Instead, it identifies a set of underlying capabilities that can be applied across changing media and contexts.

Graphic designers work between people and information.

  • Between ideas and forms.
  • Between systems and experiences.
  • Between technologies and their uses.
  • Between organizational intentions and human needs.

This intermediary position has always been part of graphic design. What has changed is the complexity and scale of the environments in which that work now occurs.

What should students learn?

If we accept that graphic design is larger than its outputs, then the curriculum should make its underlying practices explicit.

A possible framework is:

Understand → Organize → Translate → Make → Test → Take Responsibility

Understand

Investigate a situation before deciding what to make.

Students learn to ask questions, conduct research, identify context, understand audiences and stakeholders, recognize relationships, and identify what they do not yet know.

Organize

Find structure within complexity.

Students learn to work with hierarchy, information, systems, patterns, relationships, constraints, and sequences.

Translate

Move ideas between people, forms, technologies, and contexts.

Students learn to transform information and concepts into forms that can be encountered, interpreted, used, and questioned.

Make

Construct something that can be experienced.

Students work with typography, image, language, motion, interaction, code, physical materials, computational tools, and other technologies appropriate to the problem.

Test

Learn from what happens when an idea meets the world.

Students prototype, experiment, critique, gather feedback, observe use, evaluate outcomes, and revise.

Take responsibility

Consider what the work makes possible—and for whom.

Students learn that design decisions have consequences and that effectiveness alone is not sufficient to determine whether something should be made.

These practices provide a potential curricular spine that can remain stable even as technologies, media, and professional applications change.

Technology as material, not destination

This framework also offers a different way of thinking about technology education.

Rather than teaching technology primarily as a collection of software skills, we can teach students to understand technology as one of the materials through which design operates.

Students should certainly develop technical fluency.

But fluency should include more than the ability to operate a particular tool.

A technologically literate designer should be able to ask:

  • What does this technology make possible?
  • What does it make difficult?
  • How does it structure information?
  • What assumptions does it contain?
  • How does it affect the people who use it?
  • How might it change the design process?
  • What are its limitations?
  • What happens when the technology changes?

This approach makes technological learning durable.

Students are not simply prepared to use today’s tools.

They are prepared to understand and learn tomorrow’s tools.

This is particularly important as computational systems and artificial intelligence become increasingly integrated into creative practice.

The goal is not to position every emerging technology as a new specialization.

The goal is to help students understand technologies well enough to make informed decisions about when, how, and why to use them.

From software proficiency to technological agency

This distinction may be especially important for the future of graphic design education.

A designer who knows how to operate a particular application possesses a useful skill.

A designer who understands how technologies work, what they make possible, what they constrain, and how to learn unfamiliar technologies possesses something more durable: technological agency.

This suggests that technology education should move progressively from:

tools → systems → relationships → consequences

Students can learn how a tool works while also learning to question the larger system in which that tool operates.

This makes technology part of design thinking rather than something added alongside it.

A curriculum organized around increasing complexity

A contemporary graphic design curriculum could therefore be understood not simply as a progression of media or software competencies, but as a progression in the complexity of the situations students are capable of understanding and shaping.

Early learning might focus on:

perception → form → communication

As students develop:

information → systems → interaction

And eventually:

systems → behavior → consequence → responsibility

The objective is not to move away from craft or making.

It is to place craft within a larger intellectual framework.

  • Students still need to learn typography.
  • They still need to make images.
  • They still need to design identities, publications, interfaces, and other forms.

But they should increasingly understand these activities as ways of investigating and shaping relationships.

A new way to communicate the value of the degree

This framework also provides an opportunity to reconsider how we communicate graphic design to prospective students, families, university audiences, and the broader public.

Instead of primarily asking:

“What can you make with a graphic design degree?”

we might ask:

“What will you learn to do?”

Students will learn to:

  • investigate unfamiliar problems
  • organize complex information
  • recognize patterns and relationships
  • communicate across different forms and contexts
  • work with emerging technologies
  • construct systems and experiences
  • collaborate across disciplines
  • test and revise ideas
  • make decisions under uncertainty
  • consider the consequences of their decisions

These capabilities can lead to many professional contexts.

Branding, publishing, information design, digital products, interaction, motion, creative technology, experience design, public communication, cultural institutions, entrepreneurship, research, and other emerging fields can all become applications of the education.

The career possibilities are therefore not the definition of the discipline.

They are evidence of its adaptability.

A possible public-facing statement

A concise public articulation might be:

Graphic Design

Graphic design is the practice of organizing information, ideas, technology, and experience so that people can understand and act.

We teach students how to investigate problems, recognize relationships, organize complexity, translate ideas, work with technology, build and test solutions, and make responsible decisions.

The tools and media will continue to change. The ability to understand, make, question, and adapt is what lasts.

This language is intentionally broader than a traditional description of graphic design, while remaining grounded in the discipline’s history and practice.

It does not suggest that graphic design has become everything.

It suggests that the discipline has always been concerned with something larger than the particular media through which it has historically worked.

Implications for our curriculum

If this framing is useful, it could become a basis for evaluating and restructuring the curriculum.

Rather than beginning with the question:

What courses or technologies do students need?

we might begin with:

What capabilities should a graphic design graduate possess?

We could then ask:

  1. Where in the curriculum do students learn to investigate?
  2. Where do they learn to organize complexity?
  3. Where do they learn translation across media and contexts?
  4. Where do they develop technical fluency?
  5. Where do they learn how technologies operate as systems?
  6. Where do they test and revise their decisions?
  7. Where do they encounter increasingly complex problems?
  8. Where do they develop independence and judgment?
  9. Where do they consider the social and ethical consequences of design?
  10. Where do students learn to explain what graphic design actually contributes?

This would allow us to evaluate the curriculum not only by its courses and assignments, but by the capabilities those experiences collectively develop.

Toward a more legible discipline

The goal of this proposal is not to rebrand graphic design.

Nor is it to argue that graphic design should absorb adjacent disciplines or reclaim territory from them.

Instead, it is an invitation to make the underlying discipline more legible.

Graphic design does not need to prove that it invented systems thinking.

It does not need to claim UX, AI, interaction, or emerging technologies as its own.

It can acknowledge that these fields have developed their own expertise and identities while also recognizing the long history of graphic design as a discipline concerned with information, communication, technology, systems, experience, and human action.

The question is therefore not:

How do we make graphic design relevant again?

It is:

How do we make visible the relevance that has been there all along?

A contemporary graphic design education can teach students more than how to make compelling artifacts.

It can teach them how to enter unfamiliar situations, understand relationships, work with information and technology, construct possibilities, test what happens, and take responsibility for what they make possible.

  • The media will change.
  • The technologies will change.
  • The professional titles will change.
  • The discipline should change with them.

But underneath those changes is a durable practice:

understanding, organizing, translating, making, testing, and taking responsibility.

That may be the foundation from which we can begin to describe—and build—the next generation of graphic design education.
