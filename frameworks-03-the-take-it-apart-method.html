The Take-It-Apart Method

A practice for turning unfamiliar things into things we can understand.

How do we investigate the thing?

Status: Working framework
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

Part of The Foundations: An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

Let’s take it apart

There is a particular kind of curiosity that begins with a simple impulse:

I want to know how this works.

Not necessarily because the thing is broken. Not necessarily because it needs improvement.

Sometimes simply because it exists.

So you open it.

You separate the pieces. You look at them. You wonder why they are there. You try to understand what each part does.

Then you start wondering what happens if you change one.

This is the impulse behind what I call the Take-It-Apart Method.

It is not a literal instruction to dismantle every object you encounter.

It is a way of approaching unfamiliar systems.

When something seems opaque, make it smaller.

Break the whole into parts. Investigate the parts. Investigate the relationships between them. Then begin putting the system back together.

The method is deceptively simple.

Its implications are not.

A personal discovery

I did not develop this method intentionally.

At least, I don’t remember deciding to.

I simply kept doing it.

As a kid, I opened things that could be opened. I organized the pieces so I could find them again. I tried to figure out what they did.

Sometimes I put everything back exactly as it was. Sometimes I made what I thought were improvements. Sometimes I used the pieces somewhere else. Sometimes I broke the thing.

Sometimes I learned more from breaking it than I would have learned from leaving it intact.

Much later, I realized that I was doing essentially the same thing with software.

I would find code someone else had written and start removing pieces:

  • Could I eliminate this?
  • What about this?
  • Does this actually need to exist?
  • Can these two things become one?
  • Can I understand why this works?
  • Could I rebuild it differently?

The same impulse appeared in design.

I began thinking of myself as a collage artist.

I rarely experienced creation as producing something from nothing.

I found things. I collected things. I studied things. I separated things. I recombined things. I changed relationships. I made something new from existing parts.

The medium changed.

The behavior did not.

Taking apart is not destroying

Deconstruction is often associated with destruction.

But taking something apart is not necessarily an act of rejection.

It can be an act of care.

You take something apart because you want to know it. You take something apart because you believe it is worth understanding. You take something apart because you suspect the thing you see is not the whole story.

The distinction matters.

The goal is not:

Destroy the system.

It is:

Make the system available for inspection.

Taking something apart does not require hostility.

We can take apart things we love, things we admire, things we depend upon, our own assumptions, and ideas we currently believe.

The method begins with curiosity rather than judgment.

From whole to parts

Complexity creates a particular cognitive problem.

When we encounter a large system, everything appears to be happening at once. The interface responds. Information moves. People act. Rules operate. Inputs produce outputs. Consequences emerge.

It can be difficult to know where to begin.

Decomposition creates an entry point.

Instead of asking:

How does this whole thing work?

we ask:

What is this made of?

Then:

What does this part do?

What does it depend on?

What depends on it?

What happens when it changes?

Each question reduces the scale of the problem.

The system becomes inspectable.

01 — Define the whole

Before taking something apart, we need to know what we are taking apart.

This sounds obvious.

It isn’t always.

Technological systems have boundaries that are often difficult to see.

Is a smartphone:

  • the device?
  • the operating system?
  • the applications?
  • the cellular network?
  • the manufacturing supply chain?
  • the app ecosystem?
  • the company that produces it?
  • the user?

All of these can be relevant depending on the question.

The first step is therefore not necessarily to establish the correct boundary.

It is to establish a useful boundary.

What am I trying to understand?

Then define the system accordingly.

Boundaries can change as understanding develops.

That is part of the process.

02 — Find the parts

Once we have a useful boundary, we can begin identifying components.

Parts can be:

  • physical
  • digital
  • conceptual
  • organizational
  • human
  • economic
  • environmental

A social platform might contain:

  • interfaces
  • databases
  • recommendation systems
  • advertising systems
  • moderation systems
  • users
  • policies
  • employees
  • servers
  • networks
  • business incentives

A classroom might contain:

  • students
  • instructors
  • assignments
  • schedules
  • assessment systems
  • software
  • physical space
  • institutional policies
  • expectations
  • cultural norms

The point is not to make the longest possible list.

The point is to make the invisible visible enough to investigate.

03 — Ask what each part does

Naming a component is not understanding it.

We need to ask what role it plays.

Ask:

  • What does it produce?
  • What does it consume?
  • What does it control?
  • What information does it receive?
  • What information does it pass along?
  • What decisions does it make?
  • What decisions does it prevent?
  • What happens if it disappears?
  • What happens if it changes?

Function turns a collection of parts into the beginnings of a model.

04 — Find the relationships

This may be the most important step.

A system is not simply a collection of parts.

It is a collection of relationships.

Ask:

  • What connects to what?
  • What depends upon what?
  • Where does information move?
  • Where does authority move?
  • Where does value move?
  • Where does energy move?
  • Where does responsibility move?
  • Where does control accumulate?
  • Where does control disappear?

Two systems can contain similar parts and behave completely differently because their relationships are different.

Understanding therefore requires moving from:

parts

to:

relationships.

05 — Follow the translations

Technologies are full of transformations.

Something enters. Something changes. Something leaves.

Follow it.

A physical action becomes data. Data becomes a representation. A representation becomes a prediction. A prediction becomes an interface element. An interface element influences a decision. A decision creates another action.

The cycle continues.

At every stage, ask:

  • What changed?
  • What was gained?
  • What was lost?
  • What assumptions entered?
  • What possibilities appeared?
  • What possibilities disappeared?

Following translation is one of the ways the invisible behavior of a system becomes visible.

06 — Find the assumptions

Every system contains assumptions.

Some are explicit.

Others are buried so deeply that they become difficult to recognize as assumptions at all.

Ask:

  • Who is the user?
  • What do they want?
  • What are they willing to trade?
  • What counts as success?
  • What behavior is considered normal?
  • What happens when someone behaves differently?
  • What resources are assumed to be available?
  • What is considered valuable?
  • What is considered disposable?
  • What is considered acceptable?

A system can encode answers to these questions without ever stating them.

Taking something apart gives us an opportunity to find them.

07 — Look for the defaults

Defaults deserve special attention.

They are assumptions translated into behavior.

A default says:

Unless you intervene, this is what will happen.

Defaults exist everywhere:

  • the default setting
  • the default recommendation
  • the default workflow
  • the default curriculum
  • the default interface
  • the default assumption about a user

Defaults are powerful because they reduce the number of decisions people have to make.

They are also powerful because reducing decisions changes behavior.

When taking apart a system, ask:

What happens if nobody makes a choice?

That answer often reveals more about the system than its stated intentions do.

08 — Find the constraints

Once we understand what a system enables, we should ask what it prevents.

Ask:

  • What cannot happen?
  • What is difficult?
  • What is expensive?
  • What requires permission?
  • What requires expertise?
  • What requires additional effort?
  • What has been made invisible?

Constraints often reveal the values of a system more clearly than its capabilities.

A system designed for speed may make deliberation difficult.

A system designed for scale may make exceptions difficult.

A system designed for consistency may make individuality difficult.

A system designed for automation may make intervention difficult.

Every system creates a landscape of possibility.

Understanding that landscape means understanding both the mountains and the walls.

09 — Ask why

Eventually, decomposition reaches a question that cannot be answered by examining components alone.

Why is it this way?

Ask:

  • Why does this component exist?
  • Why is it connected this way?
  • Why is this behavior encouraged?
  • Why is this option the default?
  • Why is this constraint present?
  • Why is this system being optimized?
  • Why does the system exist in the first place?

This is where technical investigation becomes cultural investigation.

And this is where purpose enters.

A system can be technically coherent and still be organized around a purpose worth questioning.

The tentative model

At this point, we can begin constructing a mental model.

Not necessarily a complete one.

Not necessarily a technically perfect one.

A useful one.

We can say:

I think this is how it works.

That sentence is important.

Because a model is not reality.

It is an approximation that helps us reason about reality.

The model gives us somewhere to experiment.

And experimentation gives us a way to test the model.

Break something

This is where the method becomes active.

Change something.

Remove something.

Substitute something.

Reverse something.

Disable something.

Rearrange something.

Simplify something.

Then ask:

What happens?

If the system breaks, we learned something.

If it doesn’t, we learned something.

If something unexpected happens, we learned something even more valuable.

Failure is not necessarily evidence that the method failed.

Sometimes failure is the experiment succeeding.

It has revealed a relationship we did not understand.

The value of failure

A functioning system can hide its structure.

When everything works, dependencies can remain invisible.

Break one part and suddenly the relationships become obvious.

This is why failure can be such a powerful teacher.

A broken program tells us what depended upon what. A failed experiment tells us which assumption was wrong. A collapsed design reveals which relationships were holding it together. A failed reconstruction reveals which pieces we misunderstood.

Failure creates information.

The goal is not to fail for its own sake.

The goal is to create conditions where failure can become legible.

Take it apart without losing the whole

There is a danger in decomposition.

If we focus exclusively on parts, we can lose sight of the system.

A collection of components is not necessarily an explanation.

The purpose of taking something apart is therefore not to remain in pieces.

It is to develop a better understanding of the whole.

This is why the Take-It-Apart Method naturally leads toward reconstruction.

We take apart in order to see.

We see in order to understand.

We understand in order to rebuild.

A general practice

The method can be summarized as:

Move Question
01 — Define What am I trying to understand?
02 — Decompose What are its parts?
03 — Identify What does each part do?
04 — Connect How do the parts relate?
05 — Trace What is being translated?
06 — Expose What assumptions and defaults are embedded?
07 — Constrain What does the system prevent or make difficult?
08 — Question Why is it this way?
09 — Experiment What happens if I change something?
10 — Model Can I explain the system well enough to predict what happens next?
11 — Reconstruct Can I put the system back together—or build something different?

The process is iterative.

We rarely move through it once.

We move backward. We discover something. We revise the model. We take another piece apart. We try again.

Understanding emerges through repetition.

What this is really a method for

The Take-It-Apart Method is not ultimately about objects.

It is not even really about technology.

It is a method for approaching the unknown.

When something is unfamiliar, we often have two choices.

We can defer to someone who already understands it.

Or we can begin learning how to understand it ourselves.

Neither choice is inherently wrong.

Expertise is valuable. Collaboration is valuable. Trust is valuable.

But the ability to investigate for ourselves changes the relationship.

It gives us another option:

I don’t understand this yet. Let me look.

That is a powerful sentence.

Deconstruction as a form of agency

This is why the method matters to technological literacy.

A system that appears indivisible can feel inevitable.

A system whose parts can be seen can be questioned.

A system whose relationships can be understood can be modified.

A system that can be modified is no longer entirely outside our control.

Taking something apart therefore changes more than our knowledge.

It can change our relationship to the thing.

The unknown becomes knowable.

The opaque becomes inspectable.

The inevitable becomes constructed.

And what is constructed can, at least in principle, be reconstructed.

That is the beginning of agency.

The limits of the method

Taking things apart is not magic.

Decomposition can oversimplify. Models can be wrong. Experiments can mislead. Some systems are too large to meaningfully inspect in their entirety. Some knowledge requires years of specialized study. Some consequences cannot be discovered through experimentation alone. And some systems cannot safely be broken.

The method therefore does not replace expertise.

It complements it.

Its purpose is not to make everyone an expert in everything.

Its purpose is to cultivate the confidence and curiosity to begin asking meaningful questions.

Knowing what you do not know is part of literacy too.

From taking apart to making

Eventually something interesting happens.

Once we understand enough of a system to take it apart, we begin to imagine alternatives.

What if this part were different?

What if these relationships changed?

What if this constraint disappeared?

What if this default were reversed?

What if the purpose changed?

What if the same pieces could produce a different outcome?

The act of understanding begins to generate the possibility of creation.

This is why the method does not end with deconstruction.

It points toward reconstruction.

And reconstruction introduces a different kind of knowledge.

Questions this Foundation opens

  • Can deconstruction be taught as a general learning practice?
  • What makes a system “take-apart-able”?
  • How do we decide where a system begins and ends?
  • When does decomposition clarify a system, and when does it oversimplify it?
  • How can failure be made useful and legible?
  • What kinds of knowledge can only emerge through experimentation?
  • How does changing a system change our understanding of it?
  • Can the method work equally well for physical, digital, social, and institutional systems?
  • What role does curiosity play in technological literacy?
  • How can educators create safe environments for productive failure?
  • Does understanding require the ability to modify?
  • What happens when we stop merely analyzing a system and attempt to reconstruct it?

These questions lead to the next problem.

If taking something apart helps us understand the pieces, is understanding complete once we can explain them?

Or does something else happen when we try to put them back together?

Let’s rebuild it.
