Technology

What is the thing?

A framework for understanding technology as a system rather than an artifact.

Status: Working framework

Primary researcher: Eric Andren
Research program: The Foundations
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

Beginning with a problem

We talk about technology constantly.

We talk about new technologies, technological progress, technological disruption, and whether technology is helping us or hurting us. We talk about adopting technology, resisting technology, regulating technology, teaching technology, and designing technology.

But what exactly are we talking about?

The word technology is so broad that it can become almost meaningless.

A pencil is technology. So is a nuclear reactor, a language model, a spreadsheet, a road, a printing press, a social network, and, in some ways, a classroom.

The word describes an enormous range of human-made systems, objects, processes, and practices.

And yet when we talk about technology today, we often reduce it to something much narrower:

software.

Or perhaps a device, an interface, or a product.

This creates a problem.

If we only look at the thing we interact with, we miss the system that makes the interaction possible.

Technology isn’t software

Technology isn’t software. Technology is a system of structure, translation, behavior, constraints, defaults, and purpose.

This is the working definition at the center of this inquiry.

It is intentionally broad. It is not offered as a universal definition of technology. It is a tool for looking.

A technological artifact is rarely isolated. It exists within relationships.

For example:

  • Software depends upon hardware.
  • Hardware depends upon materials.
  • Materials depend upon extraction, manufacturing, transportation, labor, energy, and infrastructure.
  • A service depends upon software, people, policies, business models, interfaces, networks, and institutions.
  • An AI system depends upon models, data, computation, electricity, cooling, hardware, labor, interfaces, organizations, capital, and users.

What we experience as the technology is therefore often only the most visible layer of a much larger system.

The interface is the tip.

The system is the thing.

The anatomy of a technology

The framework currently uses seven questions.

They are not intended to be exhaustive.

They are intended to make a system easier to see.

01 — Structure

What is the technology actually made of?

Structure concerns the parts and relationships that constitute a system.

Questions include:

  • What components exist?
  • How are they organized?
  • What depends upon what?
  • What is centralized?
  • What is distributed?
  • What is visible?
  • What is hidden?

Structure asks us to move beneath the interface.

A website is not the webpage. A music service is not the player. An AI system is not the chat box. A classroom is not the syllabus.

The visible artifact is an entry point into a structure.

02 — Translation

What does it turn into what?

Technology constantly translates.

Physical activity becomes digital information.

For example:

  • A photograph becomes pixels.
  • A thought becomes language.
  • Language becomes tokens.
  • Tokens become numerical representations.
  • A user’s behavior becomes data.
  • Data becomes a prediction.
  • A prediction becomes a recommendation.
  • A recommendation becomes a choice.

Translation is where information, material, action, and meaning change form.

Every translation introduces possibilities.

It also introduces loss.

Something is always being represented as something else.

Understanding a technology therefore requires asking:

What is being translated, and what happens during the translation?

03 — System

What other systems does it depend upon?

Nothing technological exists entirely on its own.

A technology participates in networks of dependency:

  • Technical dependencies
  • Material dependencies
  • Economic dependencies
  • Social dependencies
  • Institutional dependencies
  • Environmental dependencies
  • Human dependencies

The system question expands our field of view.

It asks us to stop treating a technological artifact as an isolated object and start seeing it as a participant in a larger ecology.

This is particularly important when evaluating consequences.

If we only examine the interface, we may never see the energy system behind it.

If we only examine the model, we may never see the labor and infrastructure supporting it.

If we only examine the product, we may never see the economic system that determines how it is built and distributed.

The system is part of the technology.

04 — Behavior

What does the technology encourage people and organizations to do?

Technologies do not merely perform functions.

They shape behavior.

A few examples:

  • A notification encourages checking.
  • A recommendation encourages consumption.
  • A search engine encourages querying.
  • A dashboard encourages monitoring.
  • A productivity application encourages measurement.
  • A social platform encourages participation.
  • An automobile encourages particular forms of movement and settlement.

A technological system creates possibilities, but it also creates patterns.

Some behaviors are explicitly designed. Others emerge indirectly. Still others become normalized simply because the system makes them easy.

This means that one of the most important questions we can ask of a technology is:

What does this system make me want to do?

And then:

What does it make difficult for me to do instead?

05 — Constraints

What does the technology make difficult, impossible, expensive, or invisible?

Every system has boundaries.

Some are:

  • Technical
  • Economic
  • Physical
  • Social
  • Intentional
  • Accidental

Constraints are easy to overlook because we tend to notice what a technology enables.

But every possibility exists alongside an impossibility.

A system designed around speed may constrain deliberation.

A system designed around scale may constrain individuality.

A system designed around automation may constrain human intervention.

A system designed around engagement may constrain disengagement.

A system designed around efficiency may constrain other definitions of success.

Constraints therefore deserve the same attention as capabilities.

06 — Defaults

What assumptions are embedded before anyone makes a choice?

Defaults are decisions that appear not to be decisions.

They establish what happens when nobody intervenes.

Ask:

  • What is selected automatically?
  • What information is shown first?
  • What requires an additional action?
  • What is enabled?
  • What is disabled?
  • What is considered normal?

Defaults are powerful because most people do not experience them as design decisions.

They experience them as the way things are.

A technological system therefore does not need to force behavior to influence it.

Sometimes it simply needs to make one choice easier than another.

This makes defaults particularly important to technological literacy.

To understand a system is partly to recognize which of its behaviors are actually choices someone made.

07 — Purpose

Why does this technology exist?

This question arrived later in the development of the framework.

It may be the most important one.

A technology can be extremely effective. It can be beautifully designed, efficient, scalable, cost-saving, time-saving, capable of automating work, and capable of generating enormous amounts of value.

And we can still ask:

Why?

Purpose asks what a system is ultimately organized to accomplish.

But it also asks:

Whose purpose?

A company’s purpose may not be the user’s purpose.

A product’s stated purpose may not be its operational purpose.

An institution’s purpose may not align with the purposes of the people inside it.

A system can therefore be highly successful according to one metric while producing outcomes that conflict with another.

This is where technology becomes inseparable from values.

Capability is not purpose

One of the easiest mistakes to make when evaluating technology is to confuse what a technology can do with what it should be used to do.

Capability answers:

Can we?

Purpose asks:

Why?

Responsibility eventually asks:

Should we?

These are different questions.

The fact that a technology can make something faster does not establish that faster is better.

The fact that something can be automated does not establish that it should be automated.

The fact that something can scale does not establish that it should scale.

The fact that something can be optimized does not establish that optimization is the appropriate goal.

Technology gives us capabilities.

Human beings decide what those capabilities mean.

The productivity problem

Contemporary technological development frequently measures success through a familiar vocabulary:

  • faster
  • cheaper
  • more efficient
  • more scalable
  • more productive

These are useful measures.

But they are not neutral measures.

They encode assumptions about what matters.

Consider the question:

What if productivity is not the right metric?

Not always.

Not everywhere.

But sometimes.

A process can become faster while becoming less meaningful.

A service can become cheaper while transferring costs elsewhere.

A system can become more efficient while increasing dependence.

An organization can become more productive while making the people inside it less healthy.

A technology can eliminate friction that was actually serving a valuable purpose.

Optimization is not the same thing as improvement.

It is improvement according to a chosen metric.

That distinction matters.

Technology is not inherently good or bad

This framework does not begin with the assumption that technology is good.

It also does not begin with the assumption that technology is bad.

Technology is a human practice.

Its consequences emerge through relationships among:

  • what we build,
  • how we build it,
  • why we build it,
  • who controls it,
  • how it is implemented,
  • what incentives surround it,
  • how people participate in it,
  • and what consequences we accept or reject.

This does not mean that every technology is morally neutral in every circumstance.

It means that evaluating a technology requires examining the system that gives the technology its behavior and consequences.

The question is therefore less:

“Is this technology good or bad?”

and more:

“What is this system doing, why is it doing it, who benefits, who bears the costs, and what alternatives are possible?”

That is a much harder question.

It is also a much more useful one.

A technology as a series of relationships

The seven elements can be understood as a chain:

Structure → Translation → System → Behavior → Constraints → Defaults → Purpose

But they are not truly linear.

They influence one another.

Purpose influences structure.

Structure creates constraints.

Constraints shape defaults.

Defaults shape behavior.

Behavior creates new data and new demands.

Systems adapt.

Adaptation changes structure.

And the cycle continues.

Technology is therefore not a static object.

It is a living arrangement of relationships.

The technology literacy test

When encountering an unfamiliar technology, try asking:

Element Question
Structure What is it made of?
Translation What is being transformed into what?
System What does it depend upon?
Behavior What does it encourage me to do?
Constraints What does it make difficult or impossible?
Defaults What decisions have already been made for me?
Purpose Why does this exist, and whose purpose does it serve?

These questions do not require complete technical expertise.

They require curiosity.

They turn an unfamiliar system from something to simply accept into something that can be investigated.

That distinction is the beginning of technological literacy.

A note on complexity

There is an understandable response to all of this:

But modern technologies are incredibly complicated. How could anyone possibly understand them?

They are.

No individual can understand every layer of every technological system.

That is not the goal.

Literacy does not require total knowledge.

We do not need to understand every molecule in a car to understand enough about a car to drive, maintain, question, or choose one.

We do not need to understand every line of code in a website to understand its incentives, defaults, and consequences.

We do not need to understand every mathematical operation inside a language model to ask meaningful questions about its training, infrastructure, deployment, or purpose.

The goal is not complete understanding.

The goal is meaningful understanding.

Enough to see.

Enough to question.

Enough to participate.

Enough to make choices.

Why this matters

The less we understand a system, the more we must rely on the people and institutions that operate it.

Sometimes that is perfectly reasonable.

Civilization depends upon specialization.

No one can know everything.

But there is a difference between trusting an expert and being unable to question the system at all.

Technological literacy creates a middle ground.

It allows a person to say:

I don’t understand every part of this.

while still being able to ask:

  • What does it depend on?
  • What is it designed to do?
  • What assumptions are built into it?
  • What are the consequences?
  • Who made these choices?
  • What alternatives exist?

That is a form of agency.

And that is where this framework begins to move beyond technology itself.

What this framework makes possible

Once technology is understood as a system rather than an artifact, the conversation changes.

We can:

  • ask better questions,
  • examine relationships rather than isolated objects,
  • see consequences that were previously invisible,
  • distinguish capability from purpose,
  • recognize that defaults are decisions,
  • identify constraints alongside possibilities,
  • and question the incentives surrounding development.

And perhaps most importantly:

We can begin to imagine changing the system.

Understanding does not guarantee change.

But without understanding, meaningful change becomes much harder.

That is why this definition of technology is not merely semantic.

It is the first step toward technological literacy.

And technological literacy is the beginning of agency.

Questions this foundation opens

  • Where does a technological system actually begin and end?
  • Who gets to define its purpose?
  • Can a technology have multiple, conflicting purposes?
  • How do economic incentives become technical decisions?
  • Which consequences are designed, and which are emergent?
  • How do defaults become normalized?
  • What happens when a system’s operational purpose conflicts with its stated purpose?
  • How much understanding is enough to constitute literacy?
  • Can someone be highly proficient with a technology while remaining technologically illiterate?
  • What happens when people understand a system but lack the agency to change it?
  • How should designers account for consequences beyond the interface?
  • What responsibilities follow from creating systems that shape human behavior?

These questions lead directly to the next foundation.

If technology is a system, then what does it mean to understand a system?

That is the problem of technological literacy.

Technology is not simply what we make.

It is the systems of relationships we create, inhabit, and reproduce.

To become technologically literate is to become capable of seeing those relationships.
