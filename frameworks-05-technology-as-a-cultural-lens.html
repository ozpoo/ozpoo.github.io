Technology as a Cultural Lens

From technology as a neutral tool to a participant in culture.

What does the thing reveal about the world that made it?

Status: Working framework
Primary investigator: Eric Andren

A part of—The Foundations:
An emerging body of research, teaching, and public inquiry into technological literacy and human agency.

THE SYSTEM IS NEVER JUST THE SYSTEM

We have spent the previous Foundations learning to look beneath the surface of technology.

We learned to see systems rather than artifacts.

We considered what it means to become literate in those systems.

We developed a method for taking them apart.

We explored reconstruction as a way of turning understanding into capability.

Now we can ask a different question.

What happens when we take apart not only the technology, but the reasons it exists?

A technological system has structure.

It has components.

It has relationships.

It has constraints.

It has defaults.

It has behaviors.

And it has a purpose.

Those things do not appear from nowhere.

Someone—or many someones—made decisions.

Someone decided what mattered.

Someone decided what did not.

Someone decided what should be optimized.

Someone decided what could be sacrificed.

Technology is therefore never purely technical.

It is also cultural.

TECHNOLOGY AS A CULTURAL ARTIFACT

Every technology is produced within a particular cultural context.

It inherits assumptions from its creators.

It responds to existing needs.

It reflects available resources.

It participates in economic systems.

It is shaped by institutions.

It responds to laws.

It reflects social expectations.

It embodies ideas about what people should do.

Even technologies that appear neutral contain decisions.

A chair reflects assumptions about bodies.

A road reflects assumptions about transportation.

A calendar reflects assumptions about time.

A school schedule reflects assumptions about learning.

An interface reflects assumptions about behavior.

A recommendation system reflects assumptions about relevance.

A generative model reflects assumptions about data, language, representation, and production.

Technology is therefore one of the places where culture becomes material.

BUT TECHNOLOGY DOES MORE THAN REFLECT

If technology simply reflected culture, the relationship would be relatively straightforward.

We make things according to our values.

The things we make reveal those values.

But technology does not remain passive.

Once deployed, it begins to influence behavior.

A road changes where people live.

A camera changes how people behave in public.

A search engine changes how people find information.

A social network changes how people communicate.

A smartphone changes expectations about availability.

A notification system changes attention.

A recommendation algorithm changes what people encounter.

Technology does not merely express culture.

It participates in creating it.

This produces a loop:

We create technologies according to our values.

Technologies shape our behavior according to their structure.

Our behavior changes culture.

That culture influences what technologies we create next.

Technology is both product and participant.

THE CULTURAL LOOP

A useful way to represent this relationship is:

VALUES
↓
DESIGN DECISIONS
↓
TECHNOLOGICAL SYSTEM
↓
DEFAULTS + CONSTRAINTS + INCENTIVES
↓
HUMAN BEHAVIOR
↓
CULTURAL NORMS
↓
NEW VALUES, EXPECTATIONS, AND DEMANDS
↓
NEW TECHNOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

The loop rarely has a clean beginning or end.

Technology emerges from culture.

Culture is subsequently altered by technology.

Neither exists independently.

This is one reason technological change can feel so difficult to control.

We are not simply choosing tools.

We are participating in feedback loops.

THE QUESTION OF PURPOSE

This brings us back to a word introduced at the beginning of the series:

Purpose.

What is a technology for?

The question seems simple.

It is often surprisingly difficult to answer.

A technology can have an intended purpose.

It can have an economic purpose.

It can have a practical purpose.

It can have a cultural purpose.

It can have an emergent purpose.

It can have different purposes for different people.

Consider a social platform.

Its stated purpose might be connection.

Its business purpose might be advertising revenue.

Its technical optimization might be engagement.

Its user’s purpose might be entertainment.

Its cultural effect might include entirely different outcomes.

Which one is the purpose?

Perhaps the more useful question is:

What purposes are operating simultaneously?

WHAT ARE WE OPTIMIZING?

This is where technological systems become especially revealing.

Optimization is not neutral.

To optimize something means to prioritize it.

Speed.

Efficiency.

Accuracy.

Profit.

Engagement.

Convenience.

Safety.

Access.

Scale.

Growth.

Novelty.

Control.

Each can be valuable.

But values can conflict.

A system optimized for one may sacrifice another.

A platform optimized for engagement may not optimize for wellbeing.

A production system optimized for efficiency may not optimize for resilience.

A communication system optimized for immediacy may not optimize for reflection.

A workplace optimized for productivity may not optimize for human flourishing.

This does not mean optimization is inherently wrong.

It means optimization always raises another question:

Why this thing?

PRODUCTIVITY AS A CULTURAL VALUE

This becomes particularly interesting in the context of contemporary AI.

One of the most common arguments for AI is productivity.

Make the work faster.

Produce more.

Automate repetitive tasks.

Reduce labor.

Lower costs.

Increase output.

These can be legitimate benefits.

But they also reveal something.

We have chosen productivity as one of the dominant measures of technological success.

That choice deserves examination.

Why should doing something faster necessarily make it better?

Why should producing more necessarily make life better?

Why should reducing the amount of human effort required to produce something always be considered progress?

Sometimes efficiency is exactly what we need.

Sometimes effort is part of the value.

A musician practicing scales.

A student struggling through a difficult concept.

A designer iterating through dozens of possibilities.

A child learning to build something.

The difficulty may not simply be friction.

It may be where learning occurs.

TECHNOLOGY AND THE DISAPPEARANCE OF EFFORT

Technology has always removed effort.

That is one of its great purposes.

We invented tools because they allowed us to do things more easily.

But not all effort is equivalent.

Some effort is waste.

Some effort is learning.

Some effort is craft.

Some effort is social.

Some effort is meaningful.

Some effort is simply necessary.

When technology removes effort, we should therefore ask:

What kind of effort is being removed?

And:

What happens to the things that effort was producing besides the final outcome?

If a technology makes writing easier, perhaps that is wonderful.

But what if writing was also how someone learned to think?

If a technology makes coding easier, perhaps that is wonderful.

But what if struggling with code was how someone learned to reason about systems?

If a technology makes image production easier, perhaps that is wonderful.

But what if the process of making images was how someone learned to see?

The outcome and the process are not always separable.

TECHNOLOGY AS A MIRROR

This is where technology becomes a cultural lens.

We can look at a technological system and ask:

What does this reveal about us?

Not only:

What does this technology do?

But:

Why did we want it to do this?

Why did we build it this way?

Why did we deploy it now?

Why did we decide this was valuable?

Why are we willing to accept these costs?

These questions can reveal cultural priorities that are difficult to see when considered abstractly.

Technology makes values concrete.

AI AS A CASE STUDY

This is where AI becomes useful.

Not because AI is uniquely important.

Not because AI is uniquely dangerous.

Not because AI is the final form of technology.

But because AI has become a remarkably concentrated cultural object.

It touches labor.

Education.

Art.

Communication.

Science.

Energy.

Infrastructure.

Economics.

Identity.

Trust.

Knowledge.

Creativity.

It has arrived with enormous investment and extraordinary cultural attention.

And because of that, it provides an unusually rich opportunity to examine ourselves.

AI becomes a case study in technological literacy.

THE QUESTION CHANGES

At the beginning of this project, the question appeared to be:

Is AI good or bad?

That question quickly became unsatisfying.

Good for whom?

Bad in what way?

Compared to what?

According to which values?

At what scale?

Over what period of time?

Under whose control?

For what purpose?

The question became less:

Is AI good or bad?

and more:

What have we built, why have we built it, how are we deploying it, and what does our relationship with it reveal?

That is a much larger question.

AI IS NOT THE WATER

Consider concerns about the environmental cost of AI.

Water consumption.

Energy consumption.

Hardware production.

Mining.

Data centers.

Electronic waste.

These are real concerns.

But describing them simply as “problems caused by AI” can obscure the more useful question.

The technology did not independently decide:

“I would like to consume this much water.”

People built infrastructure.

Organizations made investments.

Engineers made architectural decisions.

Companies pursued markets.

Governments established policies.

Consumers created demand.

Communities accepted or resisted development.

Economic incentives shaped the entire system.

The machine is participating in the outcome.

But the machine did not create the conditions alone.

The question therefore becomes:

What systems have we built around this technology, and why?

That is a technological literacy question.

AI IS NOT THE INCENTIVE

The same distinction applies to labor.

If AI is used to eliminate jobs, reduce wages, increase workloads, or intensify surveillance, it can be tempting to describe the technology as the cause.

But technologies do not possess economic objectives independently.

Organizations do.

People do.

Markets do.

Institutions do.

If an organization deploys AI primarily to reduce labor costs, the important question is not simply:

What is AI doing to workers?

It is also:

What does the organization value, and how is AI being used to pursue that value?

Technology provides capability.

Systems determine how that capability is deployed.

THE ROLLOUT MATTERS

Technology is not simply a thing.

It is also an event.

When a technology is introduced matters.

How it is introduced matters.

Who controls it matters.

Who receives access matters.

What alternatives exist matters.

What people are told about it matters.

What incentives surround adoption matters.

How quickly institutions are expected to adapt matters.

A technology introduced gradually under conditions of experimentation can produce a very different cultural relationship from the same technology introduced through an aggressive race for market dominance.

The artifact alone cannot explain the outcome.

We have to examine the rollout.

THE TECHNOLOGY / IMPLEMENTATION DISTINCTION

This distinction may be one of the most important in the entire framework:

A technology’s capabilities are not the same thing as its implementation.

The same underlying technology can be deployed toward radically different purposes.

A database can support public health or surveillance.

A communication network can support community or manipulation.

Automation can eliminate dangerous labor or eliminate meaningful work.

AI can support accessibility or intensify inequality.

The technology provides a possibility space.

Implementation chooses a path through it.

Therefore, when we evaluate a technology, we should distinguish:

What can it do?

from:

What are we choosing to do with it?

TECHNOLOGY AND RESPONSIBILITY

This distinction also changes where responsibility belongs.

If we treat technology as an autonomous force, responsibility becomes strangely convenient.

The technology did it.

The algorithm decided.

AI replaced the worker.

The platform caused the behavior.

The system made the choice.

Sometimes these statements describe genuine causal relationships.

But they can also obscure the people and institutions that designed, deployed, governed, funded, maintained, and benefited from the system.

Technological literacy therefore requires tracing responsibility through the system.

Who made the decision?

Who had the ability to change it?

Who benefited?

Who absorbed the cost?

Who had no meaningful choice?

Who continues to have agency?

These questions connect directly to the final Foundation.

THE CULTURAL LENS WORKS BOTH WAYS

Technology can help us see culture.

But culture also determines what we see when we look at technology.

Two people can examine the same system and notice completely different things.

An engineer may see architecture.

A designer may see interaction.

An economist may see incentives.

An environmental scientist may see resource consumption.

A teacher may see pedagogy.

A student may see opportunity or threat.

A worker may see automation.

A policymaker may see governance.

A community may see consequences.

None of these perspectives is necessarily complete.

This is why technological literacy benefits from multiple perspectives.

The system is larger than any one discipline’s description of it.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR DESIGN

Designers occupy an unusual position within technological systems.

We often sit between intention and experience.

We translate.

We structure.

We organize.

We make choices visible.

We create defaults.

We establish relationships.

We influence behavior.

This means designers are not merely making artifacts.

We are participating in systems.

That participation carries responsibility.

A designer may not control an entire technological system.

But they often influence how people encounter it.

The interface matters.

The default matters.

The language matters.

The workflow matters.

The affordance matters.

The thing that appears optional may not actually feel optional.

Design therefore becomes one of the places where technological literacy and cultural literacy meet.

TECHNOLOGY AS A QUESTION

Perhaps the most useful relationship we can have with technology is not one of acceptance or rejection.

It is inquiry.

A technology can become a question.

What does this system reveal?

What does it enable?

What does it constrain?

What does it normalize?

What does it make invisible?

What does it make possible?

What does it make expensive?

What does it make desirable?

What does it make difficult?

What does it tell us about ourselves?

Technology becomes a way of asking cultural questions through material systems.

THE AI PARADOX

This returns us to the original project.

AI may be simultaneously:

extraordinarily useful,

unnecessarily resource-intensive,

beautiful,

frightening,

creative,

destructive,

accessible,

concentrated,

powerful,

limited,

exciting,

and deeply misunderstood.

These statements do not necessarily contradict one another.

They may simply describe different parts of the system.

The mistake is assuming that the technology itself must be assigned a single moral identity.

A hammer is not moral or immoral.

But the construction of the hammer, its distribution, its ownership, its use, and the systems surrounding it can carry enormous consequences.

AI is no different.

The technology is a capability.

The culture surrounding that capability determines much of what it becomes.

THE LARGER QUESTION

This is why the project that began with AI has gradually become something else.

AI provided the provocation.

It forced questions that were difficult to ignore.

But the deeper inquiry became:

How do humans understand, participate in, and shape the technological systems that increasingly structure our lives?

And beneath that:

What happens when we stop treating technology as something that simply exists and begin examining it as something constructed?

Once we see technology this way, we can ask better questions.

Not simply:

Should we use this?

But:

Why does this exist?

What is it for?

What does it assume?

What does it optimize?

Who does it serve?

What does it cost?

What does it encourage?

What alternatives exist?

Who gets to decide?

And ultimately:

What kind of world are we constructing through the technologies we choose to build and participate in?

THE CULTURAL LOOP, REVISITED

We began with technology as a system.

Now we can see that technological systems exist inside larger systems.

The layers continue outward:

COMPONENTS
↓
TECHNOLOGICAL SYSTEM
↓
HUMAN BEHAVIOR
↓
SOCIAL SYSTEM
↓
CULTURAL VALUES
↓
TECHNOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENT
↓
NEW SYSTEMS

The technology is part of the loop.

So are we.

We are not outside the system looking in.

We are participants.

That realization changes the question one final time.

If we are participants in technological systems, then understanding them is not merely an intellectual exercise.

It affects what we choose to do.

And choice introduces responsibility.

QUESTIONS THIS FOUNDATION OPENS

  • How do technologies reflect the cultures that create them?
  • How do technologies subsequently reshape those cultures?
  • What values become embedded in technological systems?
  • How can we distinguish technological capability from technological purpose?
  • Who determines what a technology is optimized for?
  • How do economic incentives influence technological design?
  • What happens when technological incentives conflict with collective wellbeing?
  • How does the timing and manner of technological rollout influence its consequences?
  • How should responsibility be distributed across complex technological systems?
  • How can designers recognize their participation in larger technological and cultural systems?
  • What can AI reveal about contemporary cultural values?
  • What does our relationship with AI reveal about what we currently value?
  • Can technological systems be redesigned without changing the cultural incentives surrounding them?
  • What happens when the technology we build begins changing the values that produced it?

These questions bring us to the final movement of the series.

If understanding a system allows us to see it differently…

If deconstruction allows us to investigate it…

If reconstruction allows us to act upon our understanding…

And if technology is something we participate in rather than something that simply happens around us…

Then we arrive at a difficult question:

What do we owe one another once we understand what we are participating in?

That is the question of responsibility.

Still figuring it out.
