Field Notes
I Refuse to Call It AI

Field Notes: Working through questions about technology, literacy, systems, and the patterns we use to make sense of the world.

For a while, I resist the term entirely.

Not because the systems aren’t impressive.

Something about the word itself bothers me.

Why are we calling all of this AI?

Neural networks.

Machine learning.

Statistical models.

Computer vision.

Natural language processing.

Generative models.

Optimization.

Prediction.

Classification.

These things have histories.

Some of them have existed for decades.

So what exactly is happening when all of these different technologies suddenly become one thing?

Maybe the technology has changed.

But maybe something else has changed.

The way we are talking about it has changed.

The technology becomes accessible.

It gets packaged.

Named.

Branded.

Put behind interfaces.

And suddenly a huge collection of very different technologies becomes one cultural object:

AI.

That’s fascinating.

Because the word isn’t just describing the technology anymore.

It’s starting to do something.

The label shapes expectations.

It shapes fear.

It shapes investment.

It shapes policy.

It shapes education.

It shapes what people think the technology can do.

So maybe a name isn’t just a name.

Maybe the label itself becomes part of the technological system.

I keep thinking about that.

Because if people believe something is intelligent, that belief changes how they interact with it.

If they believe it is dangerous, they interact with it differently.

If they believe it is creative, they interact with it differently.

If they believe it will replace them, they behave differently.

If they believe it is inevitable, they make different choices.

So what exactly is the technology here?

Is it the neural network?

The model?

The interface?

The infrastructure?

The data?

The person using it?

The expectations surrounding it?

The policies governing it?

The economic system that made it available?

At some point I start wondering if I’m looking at the wrong thing.

I think I’m studying AI.

But the more I look at it, the less satisfying that object becomes.

The public conversation is everywhere.

AI is good.

AI is bad.

AI will save us.

AI will destroy us.

AI will replace artists.

AI will take our jobs.

AI is creative.

AI isn’t creative.

AI is intelligent.

AI isn’t intelligent.

AI is ethical.

AI is unethical.

AI is necessary.

AI is dangerous.

The arguments are intense.

But something about them bothers me.

They seem to treat AI itself as though it has made all of these decisions.

AI uses enormous amounts of energy.

AI consumes resources.

AI changes labor.

AI affects education.

AI creates new economic pressures.

AI changes how people work.

But…

Who decided to build the systems?

Who funded them?

Who deployed them?

Who decided where they would be used?

Who decided how they would be monetized?

Who decided what data would be collected?

Who decided what the systems should optimize for?

Who decided what would be acceptable?

Governments regulate them.

Companies deploy them.

Organizations adopt them.

People use them.

The technology participates in these outcomes.

But it isn’t acting alone.

That distinction changes the question.

I keep asking:

Is AI good or bad?

But maybe that’s the wrong question.

Not because ethics don’t matter.

Because the object is too vague.

Maybe the better question is:

What are we doing with this technology, and why?

That feels different.

It moves the question away from the technology as an isolated thing.

And toward everything around it.

Then another question appears.

If technology can participate in completely different outcomes depending on how it is designed, deployed, governed, and used…

What exactly do I mean when I say technology?

A technology can be used destructively.

It can be used beautifully.

It can solve a problem.

It can create another problem.

It can liberate.

It can constrain.

It can make something possible.

It can make something impossible.

The same underlying capability can participate in radically different outcomes.

So I start resisting another temptation:

Technology is neutral.

That doesn’t feel right either.

Technologies contain decisions.

They embody assumptions.

They create constraints.

They establish defaults.

They encourage certain behaviors.

They make some actions easier and others harder.

The interface changes what people do.

The business model changes what the system optimizes for.

The policy changes what is permitted.

The culture surrounding the technology changes what people expect from it.

So technology isn’t neutral.

But neither is it an independent actor.

Maybe I’ve been trying to locate responsibility in the wrong place.

Maybe the question isn’t:

What does the technology do?

Maybe it’s:

What happens when a technology enters a system?

A model doesn’t exist by itself.

It exists alongside data.

Infrastructure.

Interfaces.

Designers.

Developers.

Organizations.

Users.

Markets.

Policies.

Cultural expectations.

And all of these things interact.

The technology is part of the system.

But the system is bigger than the technology.

That distinction opens everything up.

Because now I don’t have to decide whether AI is good or bad.

I can ask much more specific questions.

What was designed?

By whom?

For what purpose?

With what assumptions?

What was made possible?

What was made difficult?

What behaviors were encouraged?

What became the default?

Who benefits?

Who carries the costs?

What choices were made before the user ever encounters the technology?

And perhaps most importantly:

What happens when we mistake the technology for the system around it?

Maybe that’s what I’m actually trying to understand.

I think I’m studying AI.

But maybe I’m studying technology.

Maybe I’m studying technological systems.

And now I’m not sure I know where the boundary is.

Maybe that’s the interesting part.

Maybe technology isn’t simply an object that we use.

Maybe it is a relationship between things:

people, tools, systems, environments, assumptions, and choices.

And if that’s true…

Then technological literacy can’t simply mean knowing how the technology works.

It has to include understanding where the technology sits within the system that gives it meaning and consequence.

That’s a much bigger problem.

And I think that’s where I need to keep looking.

Still figuring it out.
