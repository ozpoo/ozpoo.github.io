A Way In: A series of public essays about what it means to live in a technological world — and what it might mean to become literate within it.

A Way In — 02
What Is Technology?

I've been thinking a lot about a toaster.

Specifically, I've been thinking about what it means to understand one.

Not this particular toaster.

Just…a toaster.

If I handed you one right now and asked:

“Do you understand this technology?”

What would you say?

You'd probably laugh.

It's a toaster.

You put bread in it.

You push the thing down.

Eventually, toast comes out.

Congratulations.

You understand the toaster.

But do you?

Let's make this a little harder.

How does the toaster know when to stop?

How does it know how long to cook your bread?

What makes the heating elements get hot?

Why does the lever stay down?

What happens when it doesn't?

Could you repair it?

Could you modify it?

Could you build one?

And here's the weird thing.

You probably don't need to know any of those things to use a toaster.

And that's okay.

That's actually the point.

Because I think we've confused two very different things.

Using a technology

and

understanding a technology.

And I think that confusion has become increasingly important.

Because when we talk about technological literacy, we often imagine that we're talking about knowing how to use things.

Can you use Photoshop?

Can you use Figma?

Can you write code?

Can you use AI?

Can you operate the machine?

Can you navigate the interface?

But that's not really what I mean.

Because I can teach you how to use something without teaching you anything about it.

I can teach you which button to press.

I can give you a workflow.

I can give you a tutorial.

I can show you where the menu is.

And you can become incredibly good at using something you don't understand.

And we've built an enormous amount of our technological culture around this idea.

Don't understand the machine.

Just learn the interface.

And honestly?

That worked pretty well for a while.

Because the machines were relatively legible.

You could see them.

You could touch them.

You could take them apart.

And sometimes, if you were really determined, you could even put them back together.

Think about the technologies that surround you.

A pencil.

Paper.

A book.

A pair of scissors.

A camera.

A bicycle.

A toaster.

These are technologies.

But we don't usually think of them that way.

And I think that's important.

Because technology is not synonymous with computers.

It isn't synonymous with electronics.

It isn't synonymous with the internet.

And it certainly isn't synonymous with AI.

Technology is much older than all of those things.

A pencil is technology.

Paper is technology.

Ink is technology.

The alphabet is technology.

A grid is technology.

A printing press is technology.

A zipper is technology.

A chair is technology.

A road is technology.

A recipe is technology.

A spreadsheet is technology.

A programming language is technology.

A neural network is technology.

The things change.

The underlying relationship doesn't.

Someone figured out a way to do something.

They made choices about how that thing should work.

They encoded those choices into some kind of system.

And now that system allows someone else to do something.

That's technology.

And once you start seeing it that way…

It gets weird.

Because suddenly technology isn't something “over there.”

It's everywhere.

It's in the things we make.

It's in the things we use.

It's in the systems we live inside.

It's in the ways we communicate.

It's in the ways we organize information.

It's in the ways we move.

It's in the ways we remember.

It's in the ways we make decisions.

And eventually you start realizing something:

Technology isn't just a collection of things.

It's a collection of choices.

Someone decided.

Someone designed.

Someone invented.

Someone standardized.

Someone manufactured.

Someone distributed.

Someone sold.

Someone regulated.

Someone optimized.

Someone changed it.

And someone else inherited all of those decisions.

That's where this starts to get really interesting.

Because when I pick up a pencil, I don't usually think about the decisions that made the pencil possible.

I don't think about graphite.

I don't think about wood.

I don't think about manufacturing tolerances.

I don't think about supply chains.

I don't think about the history of writing instruments.

I just write.

And that's the miracle of technology.

Good technology disappears into our lives.

It becomes ordinary.

Invisible.

Natural.

We stop seeing the decisions that created it.

And that can be wonderful.

But it can also be dangerous.

Because when something becomes invisible, it becomes very difficult to question.

Think about a book.

You open it.

You read it.

The technology disappears.

You don't have to think about the ink.

The paper.

The typeface.

The printing process.

The binding.

The distribution network.

The choices made by the author.

The editor.

The publisher.

The designer.

You can simply encounter the ideas.

That's an incredible thing.

But now imagine that instead of a book, you're interacting with a system that is making decisions for you.

What should you see?

What should you buy?

What should you read?

Who should you hire?

What should you believe?

What should you click?

What should you pay attention to?

Now the technology isn't simply helping you do something.

It's helping determine what happens.

And suddenly, understanding the technology becomes a little more important.

Because the question isn't simply:

“How do I use this?”

It's:

“What is this doing to me?”

And maybe even:

“What is this doing to the world around me?”

This is where I think the word literacy becomes useful.

Because literacy doesn't mean you understand the entire history of the printing press.

It doesn't mean you understand linguistics.

It doesn't mean you can manufacture a pencil.

It means that when someone puts language in front of you, you have a way to engage with it.

You can read it.

You can question it.

You can interpret it.

You can respond to it.

And eventually, you can write something back.

That's the part I'm interested in.

Because I don't think technological literacy means knowing everything about technology.

I don't even think that's possible.

I think it means having enough understanding to recognize the choices inside the thing.

To recognize that there are choices.

And once you can see the choices…

you can start asking questions.

Why this way?

Who decided?

What else could it be?

Who benefits?

Who doesn't?

What happens if we change it?

What happens if we remove it?

What happens if we build something else?

And maybe—

what happens if we say no?

That's the part I keep coming back to.

Because I don't think the goal of technological literacy is to turn everyone into engineers.

It's not to make everyone programmers.

It's not to make everyone understand AI.

It's not even to make everyone like technology.

The goal isn't mastery.

It's agency.

It's being able to encounter something you've never seen before and think:

“I don't understand this yet.”

instead of:

“I could never understand this.”

It's knowing that somewhere inside that mysterious machine are choices made by human beings.

And if human beings made those choices…

then maybe human beings can understand them.

Question them.

Change them.

Refuse them.

Or make different ones.

And maybe that's what technology has always been.

Not magic.

Not progress.

Not inevitability.

Just people—

making choices—

and turning those choices into things that other people can use.

And if that's true…

then perhaps technological literacy isn't about learning how to use the machines we've been given.

Maybe it's about learning how to see the choices inside them.

Because once you can see the choices…

you can finally start to imagine something else.

Still figuring it out.
